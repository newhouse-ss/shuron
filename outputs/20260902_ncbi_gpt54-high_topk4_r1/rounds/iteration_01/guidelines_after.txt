# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases, or as a generic pathological condition/phenotype type rather than one uniquely delimited disease.  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization.

- **Use context to distinguish Specific Disease from Disease Class**
  - Annotate a disease-denoting common noun or noun phrase as **Specific Disease** when it is used in an **instance-level clinical frame** (e.g., presented, diagnosed, found, treated, or otherwise referred to as the condition of a case/patient) **or** when the full phrase is restricted by subtype-specifying modifiers so that it denotes one disease concept.
  - Subtype-specifying modifiers may include anatomic, etiologic, infectious, temporal, laterality, severity, stage, or other delimiting descriptors, **but only when the resulting phrase identifies one disease concept rather than a broad family of diseases**.
  - Broad inheritance or taxonomic phrases such as “autosomal recessive disorder” or “X-linked mental retardation syndrome” remain **Disease Class** unless the full phrase itself names one specific disease.
  - When the same surface form is used only as a **class-level/generic disease category** or **phenotype descriptor** in a definitional, taxonomic, or list-like description without patient-specific instantiation or disease-level delimitation, annotate it as **Disease Class**.

- **Generic clinical-state / pathological-process heads can still be disease mentions**
  - A noun phrase headed by a generic clinical-state or pathological-process noun (including a bare symptom noun) may still be annotated if the full contiguous phrase functions in context as an independently referred pathological condition.
  - Use **Specific Disease** when such a phrase is the condition itself being presented, diagnosed, caused, treated, or otherwise instantiated for a patient/case.
  - Use **Disease Class** when such a phrase is presented only as a generic abnormal condition, phenotype, disease-defining characteristic, or secondary pathological state at class level.
  - Do **not** apply these rules when the phrase denotes only a normal biological function, a purely mechanistic molecular event, a laboratory/biochemical finding, or a disease mention used only as a modifier for another concept.

- **Deficiency/lack/reduction-headed disorder phrases**
  - If a noun phrase is headed by a clinical abnormality term denoting deficiency, lack, loss, absence, or reduction, and is completed by a prepositional complement naming a **specific molecular entity** (e.g., protein, enzyme, complement factor, or gene product), annotate the **entire noun phrase** as **Specific Disease** when the context indicates it is the patient’s inherited or acquired condition.
  - This includes attached severity, extent, or zygosity modifiers when they are part of the diagnosed disorder mention.
  - Do **not** apply this rule when the same structure is used only to report a laboratory measurement, activity level, antigenic state, protein level, or other biochemical finding rather than a disorder.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**

**Additional examples**  
> Coats' disease of the retina (unilateral retinal telangiectasis)

- **Annotate**:  
  - “unilateral retinal telangiectasis” as **Specific Disease**

> Sjogren-Larsson syndrome is characterized by ichthyosis, mental retardation, and spasticity.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “ichthyosis” as **Disease Class**  
  - “mental retardation” as **Disease Class**  
  - “spasticity” as **Disease Class**

---

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.

- This rule takes precedence when the disease expression is **not functioning as the disease mention itself**, even if the surrounding sentence is about patients, cases, or risk.
- Expressions such as disease names modifying “protein,” “gene,” “patients,” “cases,” “kindreds,” “families,” “population,” “history,” or “risk” remain **Modifier**, not **Specific Disease** or **Disease Class**.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

**Additional examples**
- “ovarian cancer” in “ovarian cancer cases” = **Modifier**
- “fragile X syndrome” in “fragile X syndrome patients” = **Modifier**
- “Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome” in “Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein” = **Modifier**

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

- For deficiency/lack/reduction-headed disorder mentions, include the full noun phrase when it names the disorder, including attached severity/extent/zygosity modifiers and the specific molecular complement.
- **Example**: annotate “complete C7 deficiency” or “homozygous C2 deficiency” as one **Specific Disease** mention when the full phrase refers to the diagnosed condition.

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**  
   - Very general terms such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” etc. were excluded **when used alone or only as unspecific labels**.  
   - However, a fuller phrase headed by one of these nouns may still be annotated **if the entire contiguous phrase denotes a disease/disorder or disease class under the rules above**.  
   - However, the terms “cancer” and “tumor” were retained.

5. **Do not annotate references to **biological processes**  
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis.”  
   - Also do **not** annotate normal biological functions, purely mechanistic molecular events, or laboratory/biochemical findings (e.g., protein absence, enzyme activity level, mRNA stability, antigenic state) **unless** the full phrase itself is framed as a pathological condition being referred to as a disease/disorder.

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

---

## Examples

1. **“Insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus”**  
   - **Specific Disease**  
   - Prefer the whole string.

2. **CDH1 mutations predispose to early onset “colorectal cancer.”**  
   - “early onset” may or may not be part of the disease mention, depending on:  
     1. If there is a UMLS concept specifying this as a separate form of disease  
     2. If the annotator believes it should be included

3. **Human “X-linked recessive disorder”**  
   - “X-linked recessive disorder” is generally **Disease Class** unless the full phrase names one specific disorder.

4. **Her fresh serum lacked complement-mediated bactericidal activity against Neisseria gonorrhoeae.**  
   - “complement-mediated bactericidal activity” is **not** annotated; this is a functional/laboratory finding, not a disease mention.

5. **“Huntington disease” (“HD”)**  
   - The long form and the short form constitute two separate mentions.

6. **C7 deficiency**  
   - “This report represents the first cases of C7 deficiency associated with infectious complications.”  
   - “infectious complications” is too general a term; C7 deficiency is a **Specific Disease**.  
   - If the text instead says “complete C7 deficiency” and the full phrase names the disorder, annotate the whole phrase.

7. **“colorectal, endometrial, and ovarian cancers”**  
   - Considered one **Composite Mention** of several Specific Diseases.

8. **WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (“Wilms tumor,” “mesothelioma,” “leukemias,” and “breast cancer”) and nonneoplastic (“glomerulosclerosis”) disease.**  
   - Potential disease mentions include: “neoplastic disease,” “nonneoplastic disease,” “Wilms tumor,” “mesothelioma,” “leukemias,” “breast cancer,” and “glomerulosclerosis.”  
   - “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are **not** annotated because these phrases are interrupted by nested mentions.

9. **Tumorigenesis and cancerogenesis**  
   - These refer to processes, not diseases.

10. **“autosomal recessive disease”**  
    - Refers to a family of diseases that can be passed down genetically → **Disease Class** category.

11. **Large phenotypic variability with “convulsive disorders,” “motor retardation,” and “mental retardation.”**  
    - These are “grey area” terms: they may not correspond to a single **Specific Disease**, so they are annotated as **Disease Class** category if appropriate.  
    - This is especially true when they appear as phenotype descriptors in a generic or definitional list rather than as patient-instantiated diagnoses.

12. **Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome (BFLS) is a syndromal X-linked mental retardation.**  
    - “Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome” = **Specific Disease**  
    - “X-linked mental retardation syndrome” = a family of diseases (i.e., **Disease Class**)

13. **Acute meningococcal pericarditis**  
    - Exists as a separate concept in UMLS.  
    - Annotate as **Specific Disease**.

14. **Acute Neisseria infection**  
    - May or may not include “acute” depending on context.

15. **Classical galactosemia**  
    - Includes “classical” because it corresponds to a particular form of the disease.

16. **Inherited spinocerebellar ataxia**  
    - May or may not include “inherited,” depending on the annotator.

17. **“Adenomatous polyps of the colon and rectum”**  
    - **Composite Mention**

18. **Fibroepithelial or epithelial hyperplasias**  
    - **Composite Mention**

19. **Stage II or stage III colorectal cancer**  
    - **Composite Mention**

20. **Coats' disease of the retina (“unilateral retinal telangiectasis”)**  
    - “unilateral retinal telangiectasis” = **Specific Disease** because the modifiers delimit a specific disease form.  
    - “retinal telangiectasis” can also be **Specific Disease** when used as the disease form itself.

21. **Sjogren-Larsson syndrome is characterized by “ichthyosis,” “mental retardation,” and “spasticity.”**  
    - In this definitional/list-like context, these are **Disease Class** mentions rather than patient-instantiated **Specific Disease** mentions.

22. **“Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein,” “fragile X syndrome patients,” and “ovarian cancer cases”**  
    - The disease strings are **Modifier** mentions because they modify “protein,” “patients,” and “cases.”

23. **“complete C7 deficiency” / “homozygous C2 deficiency”**  
    - Annotate the entire phrase as **Specific Disease** when it is the diagnosed inherited/acquired disorder.  
    - Do not shorten the span to only the molecular name or only the head noun.

24. **“absence of the FMR1 protein”**  
    - Do **not** annotate; this is a mechanistic/biochemical finding, not a disease mention.

25. **The patient presented with persistent “spasticity.”**  
    - “spasticity” may be **Specific Disease** when it functions as the independently referred condition of a patient/case rather than as a generic phenotype label.
