# BC5CDR Annotation Guidelines

## Guidelines for disease mention and concept id annotation

### What to annotate?

1. Annotate the most specific disease mentions and select the best-matching MeSH ID. For instance, the complete phrase “partial seizures” should be preferred over “seizures” as it is more specific.
2. Annotate minimum necessary text spans for a disease. For example, select “hypertension” instead of “sustained hypertension.”
3. Annotate all mentions of a disease entity in an abstract. All occurrences of the same disease mention should be marked, including duplicates within the same sentence. PubTator provides an automatic function to help mark the duplicate mentions via a single click.
4. Annotate mentions with morphological variations such as adjectives **when the adjective itself denotes a disease/condition** (i.e., it is a conventional disease-derived form used to describe the presence of that disorder). For instance, “hypertensive” is annotated as “hypertension,” and “hemolytic” is annotated as “hemolysis/hemolytic anemia” according to the best-matching MeSH concept in context.  
   **Added moderation clarification (adjectival shorthand in outcome frames):** Also annotate a pathology-denoting adjective **even when no explicit disease head noun is present** if the adjective is being used as a shorthand label for a condition within a clinical/outcome frame (e.g., cohort labels, incidence/survival/onset/development, treatment/complication contexts). For example, in contexts like “the ischemic group,” “patients were septic,” or “progression to fibrotic was observed,” treat the adjective as a Disease mention **only if** it clearly stands for a pathological condition in context (see Rule 11 for exclusions and disambiguation).
5. Annotate abbreviations. Abbreviations should be annotated separately. For instance, “Huntington disease (HD)” should be separated into two annotations: “Huntington disease” and “HD” with the same concept id.  
   **Added moderation clarification (short all-caps clinical acronyms):** Short, all-caps medical acronyms that are used to denote a disease/condition (e.g., “ESRD,” “CRF,” “CHF”) should be annotated as Disease **even if** they appear without an explicit head noun (e.g., “ESRD patients,” “CHF development”), as long as the surrounding language frames them as patient conditions/outcomes (see Rule 11). If the acronym is instead being used as a **Chemical** abbreviation/material descriptor (e.g., a defined drug/agent acronym with dosing/administration context), do **not** annotate it as Disease.
6. Annotate all concepts in a composite disease mention using the ‘‘|’’ separator. Composite mentions such as: ‘‘ovarian and peritoneal cancer’’ should be normalized to the collection of the individual constituents as D010051 (Ovarian Neoplasms) | D010534 (Peritoneal Neoplasms). When possible, we also spell out the individual constituents with their associated text. That is, for the above example, we also provide “ovarian … cancer” for D010051 and “peritoneal cancer” for D010534.
7. Annotate a disease mention using multiple concepts to logically describe the disease mention as a whole, using the ‘‘+’’ concatenator. It may be required to represent a disease mention with multiple concepts which are not inherited with each other. For example, “bone marrow oedema” implies Bone Marrow Diseases (MESH: D001855) and Edema (MESH:D004487). Thus, the complete phrase should be annotated as D001855+D004487. When possible, we also spell out the individual constituents with their associated text. That is, for the above example, we also provide “bone marrow” for D001855 and “edema” for D004487.
8. When a disease cannot be normalized, “-1” is used (e.g. erythroblastocytopenia).

9. **Diagnosable-condition requirement (moderation principle):** Prefer annotating spans that function as a **standalone disease/condition name** in the sentence (i.e., the span itself names the condition being discussed) and can be reasonably normalized to a disease/disorder concept. This is especially important for terms that can also describe mechanisms or morphologic changes (e.g., “injury,” “damage,” “scarring,” “fibrosis,” “toxicity”): annotate them **only when they are being used as a condition name in context** (and/or are covered by the toxicity rules below), not when they are merely describing a mechanism, experimental finding, or attribute of another entity.  
   **Diagnostic head check (added moderation clarification):** If a candidate span is a noun phrase whose **head is a recognized disorder/diagnosis label** (including canonical disease nouns and nominalized diagnosis forms such as **“-osis/-itis/-emia/-opathy”** and other established condition nouns like **anemia, failure, disease, syndrome, cancer, toxicity, hemolysis, proteinuria**), then it can be annotated as Disease even if it includes **severity/temporality modifiers** (e.g., “acute,” “chronic,” “severe,” “end-stage”), provided it is used to name a condition in context.

10. **Pathological-state expressions beyond canonical diagnoses (moderation principle):** Annotate a span as **Disease** when it clearly denotes a **pathological state/condition/outcome** affecting an organism, organ, tissue, cell type, or physiological system, even if the wording is not a canonical, lexicalized diagnosis name, **as long as it is being used as a condition label in context and not merely as a mechanism or assay readout**. This includes:
   a) **Anatomy/cell + generic pathology head:** compositional phrases with an anatomical/cellular modifier plus a generic pathology head such as **injury, damage, dysfunction, abnormality, defect, impairment, lesion** (e.g., “renal dysfunction,” “liver injury,” “neuronal damage,” “endothelial dysfunction,” “podocyte abnormality”) when used to name a pathological condition/outcome.  
   b) **Pathology adjective + condition/effect/state noun:** pathology adjectives modifying a biomedical condition/effect/state noun (e.g., “ischemic injury,” “inflammatory damage,” “fibrotic changes” when framed as a condition/outcome, “necrotic tissue injury”) when used as the name of a pathological state.  
   c) **Experimental/induced model condition labels:** “agent/acronym + pathology noun” (or “agent-induced + pathology noun”) labels that are used to denote the induced pathological condition/outcome in the subjects (e.g., “CCl4 liver injury,” “LPS-induced lung injury,” “PAN nephrosis”)—annotate the **condition** span when it functions as a disease/condition mention in the narrative.  
   **Added moderation clarification (do not over-trigger on model names):** If the “agent/acronym + pathology noun” phrase is used primarily as the **name of an experimental model/system** (e.g., “X model,” “X rats,” “X group,” “marker in X”) and the abstract’s disease assertions are instead expressed via more specific outcomes/findings (e.g., “proteinuria,” “glomerulosclerosis,” “end-stage renal disease”), prioritize annotating those specific disease/outcome mentions. Do not add the model label as an extra Disease mention unless the text clearly treats it as an asserted condition/outcome being discussed in its own right.  
   d) **Important exclusions (do not annotate under this rule):** Do **NOT** apply 10a–10c when the span is primarily:
      - a **normal function/process** (e.g., “kidney function,” “membrane integrity,” “renal development”) rather than a pathological state;
      - a **non-pathological trait** or neutral descriptor;
      - a **method/assay readout, score, or measurement label** rather than a condition name (e.g., “injury score,” “damage assay,” “fibrosis scoring,” “marker of injury,” “index of dysfunction”);
      - a **purely mechanistic/molecular event** described as part of mechanism-of-action reasoning rather than as a condition/outcome in the organism (e.g., “oxidative membrane damage,” “DNA damage” in a mechanistic explanation), unless the text clearly frames it as an adverse condition/toxicity outcome being asserted for subjects.  
   e) **Specificity override:** If a generic pathological-state phrase (e.g., “severe renal dysfunction”) is used only as an **umbrella** or **summary** for more specific diagnoses that are explicitly named nearby (e.g., CRF/ESRD) and does not indicate an additional distinct condition, prioritize annotating the **specific diagnoses** and do not add an extra annotation for the umbrella phrase.  
   f) **Generic tissue-level effect vs. diagnosable condition (added moderation clarification):** If a candidate span describes only a **generic pathologic process or tissue-level effect** (e.g., headed by a non-diagnostic process noun like “damage/injury/scarring,” or appearing only as an **adjectival histology modifier** of an anatomic term, such as “fibrotic areas,” “scarred tissue,” “injured cells,” “damaged membrane”), label it **O (not Disease)**. This exclusion does **not** apply when the span is a **diagnostic/condition noun phrase** whose head is a recognized disorder label (Rule 9), even if it contains severity/temporality modifiers.

11. **Clinical/outcome-frame shorthand without explicit disease head (new moderation principle):**  
   **IF** a candidate span is either **(a)** a short all-caps medical acronym **or (b)** an adjectival stem denoting a pathological process/state, and it functions as a noun phrase or modifier within a **clinical/outcome frame** (e.g., cohort labels; “incidence of …”; “survival after onset of …”; “development/progression to …”; “treated for …”; “complication: …”), **THEN** annotate that span as **Disease** even when no explicit disease head noun (“disease/disorder/failure/anemia/-sis”) is present.  
   **Do NOT annotate under this rule when:**
   - the acronym/adjective is used in a purely **non-clinical/organizational** sense (e.g., institution names, program labels, administrative groupings);
   - it refers only to an **anatomic location** or **physiologic measurement** without pathology being asserted (e.g., shorthand for a measurement category rather than a condition);
   - it is part of a **Chemical** name, chemical/material abbreviation, or treatment reagent label rather than a patient/subject condition (e.g., an all-caps acronym defined as a drug/agent with dosing/administration context);
   - it is used only as a **mechanistic/molecular descriptor** (e.g., “oxidative” describing “membrane damage” in a mechanistic explanation) or as a **histology-only modifier** of tissue/structure without being used as a condition label (see Rules 10d and 10f).
   **Disambiguation tips:** Prefer Disease labeling when the span appears with explicit clinical/outcome triggers (patients, developed, diagnosed, complication, incidence, survival, onset, risk factors, therapy, adverse effects) and behaves like a condition label in the sentence.

### What not to annotate?

1. Do NOT include species names as part of a disease. Organism names such as “human” are generally excluded from the preferred mention unless they are critical part of a disease name. Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names are not annotated unless it is clear from the context that the disease is caused by these organisms. e.g. “HIV-1-infected” means the disease caused by the organism “HIV”. Thus, “HIV” should be included.
2. DO NOT annotate overlapping mentions. For example, the phrase “hepatitis B virus (HBV) infected” was annotated as one single disease D006509 (Hepatitis B).
3. DO NOT annotate general terms such as: disease, syndrome, deficiency, complications, etc. However, terms such as pain, cancer, tumor and death should be retained.  
   **Clarification (interaction with moderation principles):** Generic pathology heads such as “injury/damage/dysfunction/abnormality” should **not** be annotated when used as vague, non-specific language (or as a mechanistic/assay descriptor). They **may** be annotated only when they meet the **Diagnosable-condition requirement** and/or the **Pathological-state expressions** rule (Rules 9–10), or when they are valid shorthand condition labels under Rule 11.
4. DO NOT annotate references to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis”.
5. **Do NOT annotate non-diagnostic pathological processes / morphologic findings used as mechanisms or attributes (moderation principle):**  
   a) If a candidate span describes a **mechanism, lesion, morphologic change, or experimental/pathology finding** rather than a named condition—especially when its **syntactic head** is a lesion/mechanism term (e.g., *injury, damage, toxicity, scarring, fibrosis*)—label it **O (not Disease)**.  
   b) This includes cases where the candidate span appears primarily as an **adjectival/participial modifier** of another noun (or as a property of a structure/cell), rather than naming a condition itself (e.g., “damaged membrane,” “injured podocytes,” “scarring areas,” “fibrosis scoring,” “oxidative membrane damage”).  
   c) **Exception:** This rule does **NOT** apply when the span is (or is part of) a **canonical, diagnosable disease/condition name** expressed as a standalone condition term (including established disease acronyms), even if it is etymologically related to a morphologic adjective or lesion word. For example, established condition uses such as “end-stage renal disease (ESRD),” “cardiac toxicity,” “hemolysis,” “proteinuria,” and “fibrosis” (when discussed as a condition/outcome) should still be annotated and normalized appropriately.  
   d) **Additional exception (alignment with Rule 10):** This rule also does **NOT** apply when the span is a **pathological-state expression** that is being used as a **condition label/outcome** in context (Rule 10), rather than as a mechanism, assay label, or attribute.  
   e) **Added moderation clarification (generic process / histology-modifier exclusion):** If the candidate span is only a **generic pathologic process or tissue-level effect** (e.g., “tissue damage,” “membrane disruption,” “cell injury,” “structural damage of the membrane”) or appears only as an **adjectival histology modifier** of an anatomic term (e.g., “fibrotic cortex,” “scarred regions,” “necrotic areas”) and is not being used as a diagnosable condition name, label it **O**. Do not override this unless Rule 9’s **diagnostic head check** or Rule 10’s **condition-label** criteria are clearly satisfied, or the shorthand is clearly a clinical/outcome condition label under Rule 11.

### Special cases of disease annotations

1. Rules for “toxicity” related mentions. In this dataset, ‘toxicity’ and related terms are common. Below are our rules for annotating them.
   a) When the general term ‘toxicity’ appears by itself, use concept id D064420 (Drug-Related Side Effects and Adverse Reactions).
   b) When a specific type of ‘toxicity’ is mentioned (e.g. cardiotoxicity or liver toxicity), use concepts under D064420 when possible (e.g. D066126 for ‘cardiac toxicity’ and D056486 for ‘liver toxicity’).
   c) When no matching MeSH terms can be found under D064420 for a specific type of toxicity, use the corresponding disease ids. For instance, use D014786 (vision disorders) for ‘visual toxicity’ and D006311 (hearing disorders) for ‘auditory toxicity’ and ‘ototoxicity.’
   d) **Note (interaction with the moderation principle):** Although “toxicity” can be linguistically similar to a mechanism/lesion term, in this corpus toxicity mentions (including adjectival forms such as “nephrotoxic” when used to assert a toxic condition/effect) are treated as disease/adverse-reaction entities and should be annotated following Rules 1a–1c above. Do not confuse these with generic “damage/injury” descriptions that do not name a condition.
2. If the article indicates that a disease is drug induced (again common in this dataset), then one should always try to annotate the disease using terms under D064420 (Drug-Related Side Effects and Adverse Reactions) whenever possible. For instance, ‘dyskinesia’ is a movement disorder and has its own MeSH ID (D020820). However, when it is clear from the article that ‘dyskinesia’ is caused by a drug (e.g. PMID: 6727060), then we should use D004409 regardless whether ‘drug-induced’ is included in the disease mention or not. Other common examples include D056486 (Drug-induced Liver Injury) for ‘hepatitis’; D017109 (Akathisia, Drug-induced) for ‘akathisis.’

## Guidelines for chemical mention and concept id annotation

### What to annotate?

1. Annotate the most specific chemical mentions and select the best-matching MeSH concept ID. Chemicals which should be annotated are listed as follows:
   a) Chemical Nouns convertible to: -A single chemical structure diagram: single atoms, ions, isotopes, pure elements and molecules such as: Calcium (Ca), Iron (Fe), Lithium (Li), Potassium (K), Oxygen (O2),  -A general Markush diagram with R groups such as: Amino acids
   b) General class names where the definition of the class includes information on some structural or elemental composition such as: steroids, sugars, fatty acids, saturated fatty acids …
   c) Small Biochemicals - Monosaccharides, disaccharides and trisaccharides: Glucose, Sucrose … - Peptides and proteins with less than 15 aminoacids: Angiotensin II … - Monomers, dimmers, trimmers of nucleotides: e.g. ATP, cAMP … - Fatty acids and their derivatives excluding polymeric structures. e.g. Cholesterol, glycerol, prostaglandin E1 …
   d) Synthetic Polymers such as: Polyethylene glycol
   e) Special chemicals having well-defined chemical compositions. E.g. “ethanolic extract of Daucus carota seeds (DCE)” in 16755009; “grape seed proanthocyanidin extract” in 11334364.
2. Annotate all mentions of a chemical entity in an abstract.
3. Annotate abbreviations. Some abbreviations are ambiguous by convention. Take “Nitric Oxide (NO)” as an example, “NO” could also be interpreted as a negative response. Ambiguity should be avoided using context.  
   **Added moderation clarification (Chemical vs Disease acronyms):** All-caps abbreviations may also be used for diseases/conditions (e.g., “CHF,” “ESRD”). If an all-caps acronym is defined/used as a chemical/agent (e.g., paired with dosing, administration, “injection of,” concentration units, or explicit expansion to a chemical name), annotate it as **Chemical** and do not label it as Disease.
4. Annotate chemicals when they cannot be normalized. In such a case, “-1” will be used as the concept id.

### What not to annotate?

1. DO NOT annotate other terms different from chemical nouns. Adjective forms of chemical names are also excluded. For instance, muscarinic, adrenergic and purinergic in 17244258.
2. DO NOT annotate chemical nouns named for a role or similar, that is, nonstructural concepts (e.g. anti-HIV agents, anticonvulsants, anticholinesterase drug, antipsychotic, anticoagulant, etc).
3. DO NOT annotate very nonspecific structural concepts. e.g. Atom, Ion, Molecular, Lipid, Protein …
4. DO NOT annotate words that are not chemicals in context, even if they are co-incidentally the same set of characters (synonyms and metaphors). For instance, “Gold” should not be annotated if it appears in “gold standard.”
5. DO NOT annotate Biomolecules/Macromolecular biochemicals: not large oligomeric and polymeric or established DNA/RNA/protein sequences. Proteins, polypeptides (> 15aa), nucleic acid polymers, polysaccharides, oligosaccharides and other biochemical are excluded. E.g. Insulin, DNA, mRNA, collagen, starch, cellulose, glycogen, lipopolysaccharide, glucocorticoid, glucagon (29 peptides), prolactin (199 peptides) …
6. DO NOT annotate general vague compositions. For instance, according to Wikipedia, the term opiate describes any of the narcotic opioid alkaloids found as natural products in the opium poppy plant, Papaver somniferum, and thus should be excluded.
7. DO NOT annotate special words not to be labeled by convention (e.g. Water, saline, juice, etc).

### Special cases of chemical annotations

- Antidepressive Agents (D000928), Estrogens (D004967), DContraceptives, Oral (D003276) are annotated in order to match CTD’s relation annotations.
- For combo drugs, mark as one entity and use their corresponding id in MeSH when possible (as opposed to annotating its component separately). For example, select levodopa/carbidopa in PMID:2265898 and assign MeSH ID:C009265 (carbidopa, levodopa drug combination).
