# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease, disorder, or disease-like pathological state may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases, that refer to a disease type/category in a general, taxonomic, or definitional sense, or that denote a pathological state/manifestation at a class level (e.g., symptom complex, developmental abnormality, dysfunction, deficiency, loss, or absence) when the context presents it as a disease-defining feature, consequence, or etiologic abnormality rather than as a mere finding or attribute.  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization, or that are contextually specific enough to denote the particular disorder under discussion.  
- When a mention is headed by a lexically generic or otherwise broad disease noun, use contextual narrowing to decide between **Specific Disease** and **Disease Class**:  
  - If the phrase is narrowed by a restrictive modifier (e.g., etiologic, anatomic, temporal/chronicity, genetic, laterality, or other subtype-defining modification), annotate it as **Specific Disease** when it identifies a particular disorder.  
  - If the phrase appears in a patient-, subject-, or case-level diagnosis/presentation frame and clearly identifies the disorder affecting that individual or case, annotate it as **Specific Disease**.  
  - Do **not** apply the above rule when the phrase is used in an unanchored taxonomic, definitional, or general class-level sense. In that situation, annotate it as **Disease Class**.  
- Pathological-state mentions that lack a canonical disease-name head may still be annotated as **Disease Class** when the phrase itself denotes an abnormal condition with independent disease-state meaning and appears in a disease-defining context such as a description of features, consequences, or etiology of a disorder (e.g., phrases introduced by “characterized by,” “associated with,” “results in,” or “caused by”).  
- This can include some symptom, developmental-abnormality, dysfunction, deficiency, loss, or absence phrases when they function as stand-alone pathological-state entities.  
- Do **not** apply the pathological-state rule when the phrase denotes a normal biological process, a generic experimental or physiologic finding, a mechanistic molecular readout, or serves only as a non-entity modifier/attribute without independent disease-state meaning.  
- Phrases headed by process or readout nouns such as “development,” “activation,” “expression,” “methylation,” “angiogenesis,” “vasculogenesis,” or “activity” are **not** annotated solely because they are modified by abnormal/pathologic language; annotate them only if the full phrase clearly denotes an abnormal disease-state entity rather than the process or finding itself.  
- This rule only resolves **Specific Disease** vs **Disease Class**. If the disease string is functioning as a modifier of another concept, use the **Modifier** category instead (see Rule 3).  
- This rule does not convert symptoms, findings, phenotypes, or biological processes into **Specific Disease** mentions unless they otherwise satisfy the disease criteria; some such mentions may instead be annotated as **Disease Class** when they meet the pathological-state criteria above.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**

> Sjogren-Larsson syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by ichthyosis.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Sjogren-Larsson syndrome” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disorder” as **Disease Class**  
  - “ichthyosis” as **Disease Class**

---

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.  
- This category takes precedence whenever the disease string is syntactically modifying another concept, even if the surrounding sentence is about cases, patients, populations, studies, proteins, genes, families, or kindreds.  
- The same precedence applies to disease-class and pathological-state strings: if the string is functioning as a modifier of another concept rather than as an independent disease/state mention, annotate it as **Modifier**.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.  
- Include restrictive modifiers only when they are part of the subtype-defining or otherwise most specific disease mention.  
- Exclude contextual words that do not belong to the disease name or disease form being expressed.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate bare/general terms**  
   - Very general terms such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” etc. were excluded when used alone or in a broad, unanchored sense.  
   - However, if such a head noun is sufficiently narrowed by restrictive modifiers or by concrete diagnosis context, annotate the full phrase and classify it using Rule 1.  
   - Likewise, phrases headed by nouns such as “deficiency,” “loss,” “absence,” “dysfunction,” or “abnormality” may be annotated only when the full span denotes an independent pathological state in disease-defining context; leave them unannotated when they remain broad, generic, mechanistic, or merely descriptive.  
   - The terms “cancer” and “tumor” were retained when they function as disease mentions; classify them as **Specific Disease** or **Disease Class** depending on context.

5. **Do not annotate references to biological processes**  
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis.”  
   - Also do not annotate normal biological processes or generic experimental/physiologic findings, even when they appear in a disease discussion. This includes process or readout phrases such as “retinal vasculogenesis,” “activation,” “expression,” “methylation,” or “enzyme activity” unless the text clearly uses the full phrase as an abnormal disease-state entity covered by Rule 1.

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

---

## Examples

1. **“Insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus”**  
   - **Specific Disease**  
   - Prefer the whole string.

2. **CDH1 mutations predispose to early onset “colorectal cancer.”**  
   - “early onset” may or may not be part of the disease mention, depending on:  
     1. If there is a UMLS concept specifying this as a separate form of disease  
     2. If the annotator believes it should be included

3. **Human “X-linked recessive disorder”**

4. **Her fresh serum lacked complement-mediated bactericidal activity against Neisseria gonorrhoeae.**

5. **“Huntington disease” (“HD”)**  
   - The long form and the short form constitute two separate mentions.

6. **C7 deficiency**  
   - “This report represents the first cases of C7 deficiency associated with infectious complications.”  
   - “infectious complications” is too general a term; C7 deficiency is a **Specific Disease**.

7. **“colorectal, endometrial, and ovarian cancers”**  
   - Considered one **Composite Mention** of several Specific Diseases.

8. **WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (“Wilms tumor,” “mesothelioma,” “leukemias,” and “breast cancer”) and nonneoplastic (“glomerulosclerosis”) disease.**  
   - Potential disease mentions include: “neoplastic disease,” “nonneoplastic disease,” “Wilms tumor,” “mesothelioma,” “leukemias,” “breast cancer,” and “glomerulosclerosis.”  
   - “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are **not** annotated because these phrases are interrupted by nested mentions.

9. **Tumorigenesis and cancerogenesis**  
   - These refer to processes, not diseases.

10. **“autosomal recessive disease”**  
    - Refers to a family of diseases that can be passed down genetically → **Disease Class**  
    - The same type of generic-headed phrase remains **Disease Class** when used definitionally or taxonomically rather than to denote a concrete diagnosed disorder.

11. **Large phenotypic variability with “convulsive disorders,” “motor retardation,” and “mental retardation.”**  
    - These are “grey area” pathological-state terms: annotate them as **Disease Class** when they function as stand-alone abnormal condition mentions in context, rather than merely as broad descriptive attributes.

12. **Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome (BFLS) is a syndromal X-linked mental retardation.**  
    - “Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome” = **Specific Disease**  
    - “X-linked mental retardation syndrome” = a family of diseases (i.e., **Disease Class**)

13. **Acute meningococcal pericarditis**  
    - Exists as a separate concept in UMLS.

14. **Acute Neisseria infection**  
    - May or may not include “acute” depending on context.

15. **Classical galactosemia**  
    - Includes “classical” because it corresponds to a particular form of the disease.

16. **Inherited spinocerebellar ataxia**  
    - May or may not include “inherited,” depending on whether it functions as subtype-defining specificity in context.

17. **“Adenomatous polyps of the colon and rectum”**  
    - **Composite Mention**

18. **Fibroepithelial or epithelial hyperplasias**  
    - **Composite Mention**

19. **Stage II or stage III colorectal cancer**  
    - **Composite Mention**

20. **“retinal telangiectasis” and “unilateral retinal telangiectasis”**  
    - **Specific Disease** when the anatomic/laterality modifiers identify the particular retinal disorder under discussion rather than a broad class.

21. **“ovarian cancer cases,” “fragile X syndrome patients,” and “Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein”**  
    - The disease strings are **Modifier** because they modify another noun; do not relabel them as **Specific Disease** solely because the surrounding context is case-, patient-, or subject-level.

22. **“cancer” in “family history of cancer”**  
    - **Disease Class** because it denotes cancer in a general class-level sense rather than one specific disease.

23. **“autosomal recessive disorder” in “Sjogren-Larsson syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder.”**  
    - **Disease Class** because the phrase is used definitionally/class-level, not as a concrete diagnosis mention.

24. **“ichthyosis” in “Sjogren-Larsson syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by ichthyosis.”**  
    - “ichthyosis” = **Disease Class** because it denotes an abnormal pathological state used as a disease-defining feature.

25. **“abnormal retinal vascular development” in “Coats disease is characterized by abnormal retinal vascular development.”**  
    - Not annotated because the phrase is headed by a developmental/process noun and is functioning as a mechanistic description rather than as an independent disease-state mention.

26. **“absence of the FMR1 protein” or “deficiency of norrin” in molecular-mechanism descriptions**  
    - Do **not** annotate when such phrases merely describe a molecular consequence or experimental/physiologic finding rather than an independent disease-state entity.
