# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases. Population-level or feature-list phenotype labels that describe a disorder or syndrome in general, rather than a described patient's diagnosed condition, also belong to this category.
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization.

**Example**
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:
  - "Diastrophic dysplasia" as **Specific Disease**
  - "autosomal recessive disease" as **Disease Class**

**Generic head nouns with an instance anchor**
A mention whose head noun is a generic category noun (an inflammatory, infectious, or deficiency-type noun with no proper name, such as "infection," "pericarditis," or "deficiency," including plurals) is not a **Disease Class** merely because its head is generic. Such a mention is annotated as **Specific Disease** (covering the full mention) when it is anchored to one identifiable case-level condition through at least one of the following:
- (a) a gene symbol, organism, or pathogen modifier that identifies that condition (e.g., "C7 deficiency," "Neisseria gonorrhoeae infection");
- (b) an anatomical or etiologic modifier applied to the described patient's condition (e.g., "left hip infection");
- (c) a case-attributing predicate whose subject is the described patient, i.e., a diagnostic or presentational verb or participle phrase (e.g., "presenting with X," "found to have X"). The predicate provides the anchor but is not part of the annotated span.

The following limits apply:
- **Negative constraint:** Do not apply this rule to population-level or feature-list phenotype labels that describe a disorder or syndrome in general and are not attributed to a described patient's diagnosed condition. These remain **Disease Class**.
- Do not apply this rule when no instance anchor is present. Unanchored generic terms are handled by the general-term exclusion under "What NOT to Annotate."
- Proper-named or eponymic mentions (e.g., "Coats disease") are governed by their name and need no anchor.
- This rule covers only inflammatory-, infectious-, and deficiency-type head nouns. Other feature labels, such as "mental retardation" or "convulsive disorders," are classified by the **Disease Class** examples below, except that the absence, deficient-activity, and disorder-class suffix nominals described in the following subsection are handled there.
- **Scope:** This rule refines the **Specific Disease** / **Disease Class** boundary only. It does not change the span boundaries or categories of **Modifier** or **Composite Mention** annotations (Sections 2 and 3).

**Disorder-denoting state or finding nominals in a disease-defining slot**
A mention is annotated as **Specific Disease** over its full span, rather than left unannotated, when its head is either:
- a state nominal that names a disorder, i.e., an absence, deficiency, or deficient-activity noun governing a single defined biological component (e.g., "deficient activity of fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase," "absence of the FMR1 protein"); or
- a disorder-class suffix form (e.g., "-osis," "-itis," "-emia," "-penia") used as a finding noun,

and the span fills the disease-defining slot of its clause and is not embedded in a consequence frame.

- **Disease-defining slot:** The span is either (i) the subject of a disease-topic predication about a described patient's condition, or (ii) the complement of a characterizing, etiological, or presentational predicate that attributes the condition to the described patient (e.g., "characterized by," "caused by," "presenting with," "was found to have," "in a patient with"). The predicate is not part of the annotated span.
- **Consequence frame:** The span is the object or complement of a consequence predicate ("results in," "resulting in," "resulted in," "leads to," "led to," "causes"). This holds whether or not the subject of that predicate is an annotated disease.

**Does not apply when:**
1. The state nominal is in a consequence frame, as defined above. It is a downstream mechanism and stays unannotated.
2. The span is a bare sign or symptom noun with no disorder-denoting head (e.g., "spasticity," "telangiectasis" used as a finding). It stays unannotated and is flagged for guideline review rather than forced to a disease label.
3. The span modifies or classifies a disorder without denoting one in its own right. Route it to the Modifier rule (Section 3) or the **Disease Class** rule instead.
4. The span is a lexicalized disease name (a disorder noun such as "disease," "syndrome," or "cancer," or an established acronym). Standard **Specific Disease** labeling applies, and this subsection is not needed.
5. The state nominal is one item in a coordinated feature list within a general description of a disorder or syndrome (e.g., "X is an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by ichthyosis, mental retardation, spasticity, and deficient activity of fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase"). A general characterization of this kind does not fill a case-level disease-defining slot. The list items remain population-level feature-list labels under the feature-list negative constraint above, and this subsection does not promote them. The same phrase in a case-level presentation is annotated (see Example 26).
6. The span is a parenthetical or appositive gloss that describes an already-annotated disease rather than naming it (e.g., "(unilateral retinal telangiectasis)" following "Coats' disease"). It is a description, not the slot filler, and stays unannotated.

- **Relation to the generic-head rule:** Where an anchored deficiency-type noun also satisfies this subsection, the outcome is the same: **Specific Disease**. This subsection extends the same logic to absence and deficient-activity nouns anchored by a defined component rather than by a gene symbol, organism, or modifier, and to disorder-class suffix findings.
- **Scope:** Like the generic-head rule, this subsection refines the **Specific Disease** / unannotated boundary only. It does not change the span boundaries or categories of **Modifier** or **Composite Mention** annotations (Sections 2 and 3), except for the state-nominal span guidance in Section 5.

---

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

**Example**
> The text phrase "Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy" refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: "Duchenne" and "Becker muscular dystrophy," it results in information loss, because the word "Duchenne" on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.

**Example**
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:
  - "colorectal cancer" as **Modifier**
  - "HNPCC" as **Modifier**

The generic-head rule in Section 1 affects only the **Specific Disease** / **Disease Class** label. It does not alter the span or category decisions for **Modifier** annotations. The state-nominal subsection in Section 1 does the same.

**Disease terms premodifying molecular or biological-entity heads**
When a disease-denoting token sequence (a full-name phrase or an acronym) directly premodifies a head noun from the molecular or biological-entity class (such as gene, protein, enzyme, mutation, variant, allele, or polymorphism), annotate only the disease-denoting tokens as **Modifier**. Keep the head noun, locus numerals, and any parenthetical symbols outside the span. Disease tokens are not assigned O merely because the surrounding nomenclature reads as a molecular identifier.

- **Trigger:** The disease term must directly premodify the head. Between the two, there may be only a short run of locus numerals, hyphenated affixes, or nomenclature suffixes. A content word in between (e.g., "susceptibility" in "breast cancer susceptibility gene") means the disease term modifies that word rather than the head, and the trigger is not met.
- **Scope of the label:** The label depends on the syntactic relation (direct premodifier of a head) and the semantic class of that head. It applies to any disease-denoting premodifier of any molecular-entity head, regardless of the specific disease, locus number, or acronym, and regardless of whether the string resembles a gene symbol or proper name.
- **General terms stay excluded:** "Disease," "syndrome," and similar general terms remain excluded under "What NOT to Annotate" (item 4). "Disease genes" or "a disease locus" therefore receives no annotation.
- **Negative constraints.** Do not apply this subsection when:
  - (a) the disease term is the standalone referent, such as an acronym defined in parentheses or a bare disease noun phrase with no molecular head. Such mentions are **Specific Disease** (Section 6). The parenthetical acronym in "diastrophic dysplasia (DTD)" therefore remains **Specific Disease**. Later uses of that acronym as a direct premodifier are handled by this subsection;
  - (b) the head noun is clinical or epidemiological, such as cases, patients, families, risk, population, or study. The ordinary Modifier rule applies;
  - (c) the string contains no disease-denoting content and is a pure molecular identifier. Tag O;
  - (d) the disease term is not a direct premodifier but appears inside a prepositional or relative phrase attached to the head. The ordinary Modifier rule applies;
  - (e) the disease term is coordinated with another term. The Composite Mention rule (Section 2) governs.
- **Official gene names are excepted.** A full gene name, or the expansion of an approved gene symbol, that contains disease words is a gene identifier and is not annotated (see "What NOT to Annotate," item 7). In "breast cancer susceptibility gene 1 (BRCA1)," which is the official name of BRCA1, nothing is annotated. This is the one case in which a disease term inside a molecular-entity phrase is left unannotated.
- **Examples:** In "ancient SLS genes," "SLS" is **Modifier**, and "genes" stays outside the span. In "the DTD gene," "DTD" is **Modifier**, and "gene" stays outside the span.

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.

**Example**
> In the case of "insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus," the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as "diabetes mellitus" or "diabetes."

For anchored generic-head mentions (Section 1), the span includes the generic head and its anchoring gene, organism, pathogen, anatomical, or etiologic modifier. It does not include the predicate that attributes the condition to the patient.

For state-nominal mentions governed by the Section 1 subsection, the span runs from the state head through its complement (e.g., "deficient activity of fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase"). A parenthetical symbol naming the component (e.g., "(FALDH)") and the predicate that attributes the condition to the patient (e.g., "presenting with") are outside the span.

For **Modifier** mentions under Section 3 that premodify a molecular-entity head, the span covers only the disease-denoting tokens (e.g., "SLS" in "SLS genes"). The head noun is excluded.

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as "Huntington disease" ("HD") are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation). Later uses of such an acronym as a direct premodifier of a molecular-entity head follow Section 3.

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**
   - Organism names such as "human" were excluded from the preferred mention.
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.
   - Where an organism or pathogen modifies a generic infectious head noun that is anchored to a described patient's condition (see Section 1), the whole phrase (e.g., "Epstein-Barr virus infection") is annotated as **Specific Disease**. This rule does not, by itself, make a bare organism name a disease mention.
   - **Example**
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.

     In this case: "Epstein-Barr virus" and "cytomegalovirus" are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not. Here, no disease is attributed to a patient, so neither is annotated.

2. **Do not annotate gender**
   - Tokens such as "male" and "female" were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.
   - **Example**
     > "male breast cancer"

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**
   - **Example**: The phrase "von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease" is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**
   - Very general terms such as "disease," "syndrome," "deficiency," "complications," "abnormalities," etc. were excluded when they appear without an instance anchor.
   - This includes general terms used as premodifiers of molecular heads, such as "disease gene" or "disease locus."
   - A generic inflammatory, infectious, or deficiency-type head noun that is anchored to a described patient's identifiable condition is not excluded. It is annotated as **Specific Disease** under Section 1 of "What to Annotate" (e.g., "left hip infection" in a patient who was admitted with it).
   - Generic terms outside that class (e.g., "disease," "syndrome," "complications") remain excluded when used alone. A proper name that contains such a word, such as "Huntington disease" or "Coats disease," is governed by its name.
   - However, the terms "cancer" and "tumor" were retained.
   - Bare state nominals that do not fill a disease-defining slot, and sign or symptom nouns with no disorder-denoting head, are excluded. Sign or symptom nouns of this kind are left unannotated and flagged for guideline review rather than forced to a disease label. Mechanisms introduced by a consequence predicate (e.g., "results in a deficiency of norrin") are excluded in the same way.

5. **Do not annotate references to **biological processes**
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as "tumorigenesis" or "cancerogenesis."

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.
   - **Example**
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.

     Here, the mentions "neoplastic disease" and "nonneoplastic disease" are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

7. **Do not annotate official gene names**
   - A disease word that is part of an official gene name (a full gene name or the expansion of an approved gene symbol) belongs to a gene identifier and is not annotated.
   - **Example**: "breast cancer susceptibility gene 1 ( BRCA1 )" is not annotated.
   - This is the only exception to the molecular-head rule in Section 3. Proper disease names such as "Coats disease" are not gene names and remain governed by Section 1.

**NEW MODERATION PRINCIPLES:**
- **Disease Premodifier of a Molecular Head Noun**
  - **IF** a span denoting a disease or disorder appears directly before a head noun that names a molecular entity (such as a gene, protein, or other biomolecule), including when the compound is followed by a parenthesized symbol or acronym,
  - **THEN** label the disease-denoting span as `Modifier`, and label the molecular head noun, its numerals or nomenclature tokens, and any parenthesized identifier as `O`. The molecular status of the head must not suppress the disease label.
  - **Does NOT apply when:** (a) the disease-denoting span follows the head noun in a prepositional or relative phrase rather than premodifying it; (b) the premodifying span denotes a non-disease category, such as an organism, anatomical site, biological process, or chemical; (c) the disease-denoting string is a single undivided identifier token with no separable disease word; or (d) the head noun is clinical or population-level (e.g., patients, cases, population, phenotype), where the same disease-premodifier labeling already applies and this rule adds no new constraint.
