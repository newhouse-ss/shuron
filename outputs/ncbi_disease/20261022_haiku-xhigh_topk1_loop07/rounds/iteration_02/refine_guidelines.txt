# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases. Population-level or feature-list phenotype labels that describe a disorder or syndrome in general, rather than a described patient's diagnosed condition, also belong to this category.
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization.

**Example**
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:
  - "Diastrophic dysplasia" as **Specific Disease**
  - "autosomal recessive disease" as **Disease Class**

**Generic head nouns with an instance anchor**
A mention whose head noun is a generic category noun (an inflammatory, infectious, or deficiency-type noun with no proper name, such as "infection," "pericarditis," or "deficiency," including plurals) is not a **Disease Class** merely because its head is generic. Such a mention is annotated as **Specific Disease** (covering the full mention) when it is anchored to one identifiable case-level condition through at least one of the following:
- (a) a gene symbol, organism, or pathogen modifier that identifies that condition (e.g., "C7 deficiency," "Neisseria gonorrhoeae infection");
- (b) an anatomical or etiologic modifier applied to the described patient's condition (e.g., "left hip infection");
- (c) a case-attributing predicate whose subject is the described patient, i.e., a diagnostic or presentational verb or participle phrase (e.g., "presenting with X," "found to have X"). The predicate provides the anchor but is not part of the annotated span.

The following limits apply:
- **Negative constraint:** Do not apply this rule to population-level or feature-list phenotype labels that describe a disorder or syndrome in general and are not attributed to a described patient's diagnosed condition. These remain **Disease Class**.
- Do not apply this rule when no instance anchor is present. Unanchored generic terms are handled by the general-term exclusion under "What NOT to Annotate."
- Proper-named or eponymic mentions (e.g., "Coats disease") are governed by their name and need no anchor.
- This rule covers only inflammatory-, infectious-, and deficiency-type head nouns. Other feature labels, such as "mental retardation" or "convulsive disorders," are classified by the **Disease Class** examples below.
- **Scope:** This rule refines the **Specific Disease** / **Disease Class** boundary only. It does not change the span boundaries or categories of **Modifier** or **Composite Mention** annotations (Sections 2 and 3).

---

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

**Example**
> The text phrase "Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy" refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: "Duchenne" and "Becker muscular dystrophy," it results in information loss, because the word "Duchenne" on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.

**Example**
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:
  - "colorectal cancer" as **Modifier**
  - "HNPCC" as **Modifier**

The generic-head rule in Section 1 affects only the **Specific Disease** / **Disease Class** label. It does not alter the span or category decisions for **Modifier** annotations.

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.

**Example**
> In the case of "insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus," the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as "diabetes mellitus" or "diabetes."

For anchored generic-head mentions (Section 1), the span includes the generic head and its anchoring gene, organism, pathogen, anatomical, or etiologic modifier. It does not include the predicate that attributes the condition to the patient.

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as "Huntington disease" ("HD") are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**
   - Organism names such as "human" were excluded from the preferred mention.
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.
   - Where an organism or pathogen modifies a generic infectious head noun that is anchored to a described patient's condition (see Section 1), the whole phrase (e.g., "Epstein-Barr virus infection") is annotated as **Specific Disease**. This rule does not, by itself, make a bare organism name a disease mention.
   - **Example**
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.

     In this case: "Epstein-Barr virus" and "cytomegalovirus" are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not. Here, no disease is attributed to a patient, so neither is annotated.

2. **Do not annotate gender**
   - Tokens such as "male" and "female" were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.
   - **Example**
     > "male breast cancer"

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**
   - **Example**: The phrase "von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease" is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**
   - Very general terms such as "disease," "syndrome," "deficiency," "complications," "abnormalities," etc. were excluded when they appear without an instance anchor.
   - A generic inflammatory, infectious, or deficiency-type head noun that is anchored to a described patient's identifiable condition is not excluded. It is annotated as **Specific Disease** under Section 1 of "What to Annotate" (e.g., "left hip infection" in a patient who was admitted with it).
   - Generic terms outside that class (e.g., "disease," "syndrome," "complications") remain excluded when used alone. A proper name that contains such a word, such as "Huntington disease" or "Coats disease," is governed by its name.
   - However, the terms "cancer" and "tumor" were retained.

5. **Do not annotate references to **biological processes**
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as "tumorigenesis" or "cancerogenesis."

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.
   - **Example**
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.

     Here, the mentions "neoplastic disease" and "nonneoplastic disease" are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

1. **"Insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus"**

   - Prefer the whole string.

2. **CDH1 mutations predispose to early onset "colorectal cancer."**
   - "early onset" may or may not be part of the disease mention, depending on:
     1. If there is a UMLS concept specifying this as a separate form of disease
     2. If the annotator believes it should be included

3. **Human "X-linked recessive disorder"**

4. **Her fresh serum lacked complement-mediated bactericidal activity against Neisseria gonorrhoeae.**

5. **"Huntington disease" ("HD")**
   - The long form and the short form constitute two separate mentions.

6. **C7 deficiency**
   - "This report represents the first cases of C7 deficiency associated with infectious complications."
   - "C7 deficiency" is a **Specific Disease**. "C7" is a gene symbol, so the deficiency mention is anchored under Section 1.
   - "infectious complications" is too general a term and remains excluded. The head noun "complications" is not an inflammatory-, infectious-, or deficiency-type noun, so the anchoring rule does not apply.

7. **"colorectal, endometrial, and ovarian cancers"**
   - Considered one **Composite Mention** of several Specific Diseases.

8. **WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic ("Wilms tumor," "mesothelioma," "leukemias," and "breast cancer") and nonneoplastic ("glomerulosclerosis") disease.**
   - Potential disease mentions include: "neoplastic disease," "nonneoplastic disease," "Wilms tumor," "mesothelioma," "leukemias," "breast cancer," and "glomerulosclerosis."
   - "neoplastic disease" and "nonneoplastic disease" are **not** annotated because these phrases are interrupted by nested mentions.

9. **Tumorigenesis and cancerogenesis**
   - These refer to processes, not diseases.

10. **"autosomal recessive disease"**
    - Refers to a family of diseases that can be passed down genetically → **Disease Class** category.

11. **Large phenotypic variability with "convulsive disorders," "motor retardation," and "mental retardation."**
    - These are "grey area" terms: they may not correspond to a single **Specific Disease**, so they are annotated as **Disease Class** category if appropriate.

12. **Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome (BFLS) is a syndromal X-linked mental retardation.**
    - "Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome" = **Specific Disease**
    - "X-linked mental retardation syndrome" = a family of diseases (i.e., **Disease Class**)

13. **Acute meningococcal pericarditis**
    - Exists as a separate concept in UMLS.
    - The organism modifier "meningococcal" also anchors the inflammatory head noun "pericarditis" (Section 1).

14. **Acute Neisseria infection**
    - The generic head "infection" is anchored by the organism modifier "Neisseria," so the full mention is a **Specific Disease** (Section 1).
    - May or may not include "acute" depending on context.

15. **Classical galactosemia**
    - Includes "classical" because it corresponds to a particular form of the disease.

16. **Inherited spinocerebellar ataxia**
    - May or may not include "inherited," depending on the annotator.

17. **"Adenomatous polyps of the colon and rectum"**
    - **Composite Mention**

18. **Fibroepithelial or epithelial hyperplasias**
    - **Composite Mention**

19. **Stage II or stage III colorectal cancer**
    - **Composite Mention**

20. **"The patient was found to have a *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* infection."**
    - "Neisseria gonorrhoeae infection" = **Specific Disease**. The organism modifier (a) is applied to a case-level condition, and the predicate "was found to have" (c) attributes it to the patient. The predicate is outside the annotated span.

21. **"She was admitted with a left hip infection."**
    - "left hip infection" = **Specific Disease**. The anatomical modifier (b) applies to the patient's condition, and "admitted with" is a presentational predicate (c).

22. **"Enzyme deficiencies are listed among the features of this syndrome."**
    - "Enzyme deficiencies" is **not** anchored to a described patient's diagnosed condition. It is a population-level feature-list label, so it remains **Disease Class** and is not **Specific Disease**.

**NEW MODERATION PRINCIPLES:**
- **Principle: Disease Term Premodifying a Molecular-Entity Head Is Modifier, Not O.**
  - **IF** a disease-denoting token sequence (a full-name phrase or an acronym) directly premodifies a head noun from the molecular or biological-entity class, with at most a short run of locus numerals, hyphenated affixes, or nomenclature suffixes between them, **THEN** tag only the disease-denoting tokens as **Modifier**. Keep the head noun, locus numerals, and any parenthetical symbols outside the span, and never assign O to the disease tokens because the surrounding nomenclature reads as a molecular identifier.
  - **Negative Constraint:** Do NOT apply this rule when:
    - (a) the disease term is the standalone referent, such as an acronym defined in parentheses or a bare disease noun phrase with no molecular head (tag **SpecificDisease**);
    - (b) the head noun is clinical or epidemiological, such as a noun for cases, risk, population, or study (the ordinary Modifier rule applies);
    - (c) the string contains no disease-denoting lexical content and is a pure molecular identifier (tag O);
    - (d) the disease term is not a direct premodifier but appears inside a prepositional or relative phrase attached to the head;
    - (e) the disease term is coordinated with another term, in which case the composite-mention conventions govern.
  - **Generalization:** This applies to any disease-denoting premodifier of any molecular-entity head, regardless of the specific disease, locus number, or acronym. The label depends on the syntactic relation (premodifier of a head) and the semantic class of that head, not on whether the string resembles a gene symbol or proper name. Disease tokens inside a fixed named compound are still Modifiers, not O.
