# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases.  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**

---

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**  
   - Very general terms such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” etc. were excluded.  
   - However, the terms “cancer” and “tumor” were retained.

5. **Do not annotate references to **biological processes**  
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis.”

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

**NEW MODERATION PRINCIPLES:**
### 1. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: DiseaseClass (Label Mismatch) (6 cases)
- **Referentially Individuated Category Heads:** IF a disease mention's head is a superordinate common noun naming a disorder family (singular or plural) with no proper-name, eponym, acronym, or gene-symbol anchor, AND the surrounding context either (a) pre-modifies the head with an etiological, organism, or gene-based qualifier, or (b) predicates the condition of a specific case subject through a patient-presentation, diagnosis, or finding clause, THEN label the head mention as SpecificDisease, because it denotes one individuated condition in context. Lexical hypernymy and plural morphology are not sufficient evidence for DiseaseClass.
  - **Negative constraint:** Do NOT apply this rule when (i) the head is coordinated with, or serves as an umbrella over, several distinct named disorders; (ii) the mention appears in a kind-level generic statement describing a disorder's typical phenotype, where the term functions as a phenotype category; or (iii) neither individuating cue (a qualifier or a case-level predicate) is present, in which case the standard DiseaseClass criteria apply. The rule also does not reassign qualifier spans, which remain Modifier mentions, and does not override proper-name or acronym-anchored SpecificDisease labels.

### 2. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: O (Missed) (4 cases)
- **Principle: Privative State Nominal with Gene-Product Complement**
  - **IF** a privative state nominal (a deficiency-type noun, or a deficient-activity noun phrase) heads a noun phrase whose prepositional complement names a gene product, enzyme, or protein, **AND** the surrounding clause presents that state as the defining cause or characterizing feature of a named disorder or phenotype (for example, a "caused by" or "characterized by" construction, or a head-first construction that names the disorder), **THEN** label the full span, including the state noun, the preposition, and the protein complement, as SpecificDisease.
  - **Does NOT apply when:**
    - The state nominal is the effect of a mechanism, introduced by a result or causal-consequence verb with no disease-defining frame. Label the state and the protein as O.
    - The complement is a bare gene or protein mention with no state nominal heading it.
    - The item is a single-word sign or symptom (e.g., a fever or a spasticity-type finding), even inside a disease-defining list. Keep these O. List position alone does not make an item a disease.
    - The state nominal is a fixed premodifier-plus-head compound (e.g., [gene] + deficiency). Label that as SpecificDisease under the lexicalized disease-name convention. It does not need the disease-defining frame, and this principle does not change it.

### 3. Gold: Modifier vs LLM: O (Missed) (3 cases)
- **Disease Name Embedded in a Gene/Protein Designation (Modifier Rule):**
  - **IF** a disease name (full, eponymous, or abbreviated) occurs as a non-final premodifier inside a gene or protein designation, meaning a nomenclature string whose head noun is a molecular term (e.g., "gene," "protein," or a numbered variant of these) and which may carry a parenthetical symbol,
  - **THEN** label the disease substring as **Modifier**, and keep the molecular head noun, index numerals, and parenthetical symbols as **O**. Assign the label from the disease token's own function within the span, not by inheriting O from the enclosing molecular name.
  - **Does NOT apply when:**
    - the disease term is the head of its nominal or stands alone as a referent to the condition (e.g., a bare disease mention keeps its SpecificDisease or DiseaseClass label);
    - the disease term modifies a human or clinical noun (e.g., patients, cases, risk, phenotype), since those are already handled as Modifier through their own function;
    - the embedded term is not a disease (e.g., anatomical, chemical, or gene-family terms);
    - the designation is purely symbolic, with no disease lexical content.

### 4. Gold: DiseaseClass vs LLM: O (Missed) (3 cases)
- **IF** a span denotes a clinical sign, symptom, or pathophysiological abnormality (a bare symptom noun, an adjective-plus-process/organ noun phrase describing an abnormal state, or a "deficiency/absence of [gene product or substance]" construction) **AND** it is the complement of a predicate whose subject is a disease and that predicate expresses characterization or causation (e.g., "characterized by," "results in," "secondary to"-type relations), or it is an item in a coordinated symptom list governed by such a predicate, **THEN** label the full span as DiseaseClass, even though it lacks disease-name morphology (e.g., *disease*, *syndrome*, *-osis*, *disorder*).
  - **Does NOT apply when:**
    - The span is an established disease name, including a "[X] deficiency" used as a recognized disease label. Label it SpecificDisease.
    - The span describes normal physiology or a general mechanism whose subject is a gene, protein, or cell rather than a disease (e.g., "required for," "regulates," "encodes"), or it appears in a treatment, assay, or experimental-condition context.
    - The span is only a modifier fragment with no standalone abnormality head.
    - The coordinated list contains a named disease alongside symptoms. Label each item by its own category and do not merge them into one span.
