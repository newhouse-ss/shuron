# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases.  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**

---

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**  
   - Very general terms such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” etc. were excluded.  
   - However, the terms “cancer” and “tumor” were retained.

5. **Do not annotate references to **biological processes**  
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis.”

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

**NEW MODERATION PRINCIPLES:**
### 1. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: DiseaseClass (Label Mismatch) (6 cases)
- **Principle: Anchored Umbrella Disease Terms**
  - **IF** a superordinate disease-category head noun (a broad common noun for a class of conditions, such as a generic inflammatory, infectious, or deficiency term) is **anchored** to one condition, meaning either:
    - (a) it is the predicate complement of a diagnostic or presentation construction whose subject is a patient or case referent (e.g., "presenting with ___," "found to have ___," "diagnosed with ___"), including each member of a coordinated list governed by that construction, or
    - (b) it carries a premodifier (a gene symbol, a pathogen name, an anatomical site, or another etiology-specifying element) that fixes a single etiology, gene, or site,
  - **THEN** label the head disease span **SpecificDisease**.
  - **Otherwise**, if the category noun is unanchored, label it **DiseaseClass**.
  - **Negative constraint:** Do **not** apply this rule when the category noun appears in a generic or definitional enumeration of features describing a syndrome or phenotype, with no case referent and no etiology-fixing premodifier. Those remain **DiseaseClass**. Descriptive premodifiers that do not identify a cause, gene, or site (e.g., severity, timing, or general evaluative qualifiers) are not anchors. The rule also does not make the premodifier itself a disease mention; the anchor determines only the label of the head disease span.

### 2. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: O (Missed) (4 cases)
- **Disease-Defining State Nominal:** IF a state or activity nominal denoting reduced or absent biological function (e.g., a deficiency-type or deficient-activity noun) takes a prepositional complement (of/in) naming a gene, enzyme, or protein, AND the same clause presents that state as the defining clinical feature or etiology of a named disorder (signaled by a predicate such as "characterized by," "caused by," or "is a disorder in which…," or by the disorder's name appearing in that clause), THEN label the full nominal-plus-complement span as SpecificDisease, because it functions as the disorder's name rather than a molecular description.
  - **Negative Constraint:** Do NOT apply when the state nominal is a downstream, intermediate, or mechanistic event, introduced by consequence or locative frames such as "results in," "leads to," or "within [tissue/cell]," or when a separate disorder noun names the disease elsewhere in the sentence. Keep these spans O. Do not extend the rule to isolated clinical signs or symptoms in presenting lists, which follow separate symptom-annotation conventions. Do not alter prenominal enzyme-modifier-plus-disease-head compounds, which are already canonical SpecificDisease spans.

### 3. Gold: DiseaseClass vs LLM: O (Missed) (3 cases)
- **Unnamed Pathological States and Clinical Manifestations as DiseaseClass**
  - **IF** a noun phrase denotes an abnormal condition, deficit, or clinical manifestation of the patient or affected tissue (including a bare sign or symptom term, or an abnormality or deficiency expressed as a noun phrase without a disease-type head noun), **AND** that phrase serves as the complement of a defining or causal predicate for a disorder, such as a characterization frame ("is characterized by ___"), a causal or outcome frame ("results in ___," "secondary to ___"), or an item in a coordinated list of features that enumerates a disorder,
  - **THEN** label the full noun phrase as DiseaseClass, even though it contains no disease head noun (e.g., *disease*, *syndrome*, *disorder*) and is not an established name. Label the whole phrase, including any embedded gene or protein name, rather than only the abnormal-state word.
  - **Negative Constraints (does NOT apply when):**
    - The phrase is itself an established disorder name, eponym, or acronym, or a fixed diagnostic term. These follow their existing SpecificDisease or DiseaseClass conventions.
    - The phrase is a bare name of an intact biological entity (gene, protein, enzyme, or molecule) with no abnormal-state head noun.
    - A disease name is used attributively to modify another noun. This is a Modifier.
    - The phrase describes normal function, healthy state, or generic mechanism or process language that is not tied to a defining or causal frame for a disorder.

### 4. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: SpecificDisease (Span Boundary Error) (2 cases)
- **Nominal deficiency with a molecular complement (span extent):** IF a nominal head denoting a deficiency (e.g., "deficiency," "insufficiency") is followed by a prepositional phrase whose complement names a molecular referent (a gene symbol, protein, enzyme, or a descriptive noun phrase naming a component), THEN tag one SpecificDisease span from the head noun through the last token of that prepositional complement, including any descriptive material embedded in it. Do not tag the complement as a separate entity, since the schema has no molecular category. Exclude pre-head qualifiers (degree, zygosity, inheritance, or severity adjectives) and post-span trailing material (patient, family, cohort, or count phrases). Where the molecular name precedes the head as a compound (e.g., "[enzyme name] deficiency"), apply the same whole-phrase span. **Negative constraint:** Do NOT apply this rule when the head is adjectival (e.g., "deficient") or functions as a pre-modifier of another entity, in which case use Modifier. Do NOT apply it when the prepositional complement is itself a disease, syndrome, or clinical-condition name, in which case the standard disease-name extent rules govern.
