# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases, or as a broader pathological category/abnormal-state, pathological-process, or abnormal-outcome class that is not anchored to one concrete diagnosed disorder or one concrete abnormal-condition instance.  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization, or that in local context denote one concrete diagnosed disorder entity or one concrete abnormal pathological condition entity rather than an unanchored category.

For borderline cases, apply the following in order:
- **Narrower rule: deficiency/insufficiency disease names**  
  If a noun phrase is headed by a **deficit/insufficiency nominalization** or a **reduced-function expression**, and its complement names a **specific biomolecule** (e.g., gene, enzyme, protein, complement component), annotate the **entire noun phrase** as **Specific Disease** when the phrase itself is being used as the name or alias of a recognized disorder in an **etiologic, mutation, diagnosis, or phenotype-defining context**.
- **Narrower rule: contextually specific abnormal-state mentions without canonical disease heads**  
  If a noun phrase—whether a **bare common noun** or a **compositional nominal phrase** with modifiers and/or prepositional complements—is headed by a term denoting a **pathological clinical condition or abnormal state**, and denotes an **abnormal clinical, physiological, or biochemical state**, annotate the **entire noun phrase** as **Specific Disease** when local context presents it as the **actual condition instance** or as a **named abnormal condition** in a **patient-case, diagnosis, presentation, characterization, causation, association, resultative, or appositional/alias frame** (for example, after predicates such as *characterized by*, *presenting with*, *causing*, or *resulting in*), even when it lacks a conventional disease-name head such as *disease*, *disorder*, or *syndrome*.  
  - This includes phrases headed by **symptom-, defect-, insufficiency-, failure-, dysfunction-, lesion-, or presenting-condition-type nouns** when the phrase itself is functioning **referentially as the condition being discussed or named**.  
  - Use this rule only when the phrase itself is functioning **referentially as the condition being discussed**, rather than merely describing **part of another disease**, listing a **generic feature/manifestation**, glossing a **mechanism**, or naming a **downstream consequence** or **local developmental abnormality** without independently referring to that abnormal condition as such.  
  - Medical framing alone is **not** sufficient: do **not** apply this rule to **normal attributes**, **tests/analytes/values**, **isolated genes/proteins/enzymes/substances**, **anatomical structures**, **quantitative or biochemical activity/readout phrases**, **purely mechanistic or causal process descriptions**, **purely adjectival modifiers**, or **broad category/class phrases** rather than the abnormal condition itself.
- **Broader rule: contextually specific descriptive disease names**  
  If a disease-denoting noun phrase has a **generic pathology head** (e.g., disease, disorder, syndrome, deficiency, infection, failure) but local context instantiates that head as the **actual diagnosis**, **condition instance**, or **one particular diagnosable subtype**—for example through **subtype-defining modification** that narrows the phrase to one concrete disorder entity, or through **case-level clinical framing** such as **presentation, diagnosis, history, evaluation, or patient-specific predication**—annotate it as **Specific Disease**.  
  - **Subtype-defining modification** may include a **specific etiologic, genetic, microbial, molecular, anatomic, or similarly narrowing dependent** when that modifier helps identify **one concrete disorder entity** in context.  
  - **Temporal, severity, quantity, certainty, or other contextual modifiers by themselves do not suffice** unless they are part of a **lexicalized disease name** or are otherwise clearly **disease-defining** in local context.  
  - This includes **lower-case common-noun pathology noun phrases** headed by generic disorder terms when they are used as the **actual condition instance** in discourse rather than as a **superclass** or **generic type**.  
  - Do **not** use this rule when the modifier still leaves the phrase as a **broad taxonomic, inheritance-based, or superclass label** (e.g., *autosomal recessive disease*, *X-linked disorder*) rather than one concrete disorder entity, or when the modifier is only a **non-defining contextual description**.  
  - When the same surface form is used only as a **generic phenotype/manifestation**, a **feature-list item**, or a **taxonomic/superclass description**, treat it as **Disease Class** instead.  
  - If the phrase is syntactically functioning as a **direct modifier** of a **non-disease head** such as *cases*, *patients*, *families*, *population*, *risk*, *gene*, or *protein*, annotate it using the **Modifier** category under Section 3 instead.
- **Broader rule: pathological-state classes**  
  If a noun phrase denotes an **abnormal/pathological physiological state, process, or outcome** and is headed by a **generic biomedical condition/state/process/result noun**, or expresses an **abnormal or deficient pathological state/process/outcome** via a **deviation-from-normal modifier** or a **defect/deficiency/failure/loss/excess/separation-type nominal construction** attached to a biological process, anatomical function, developmental process, body site, tissue, substance, or molecular product, annotate the **entire noun phrase** as **Disease Class** **only when it does not satisfy any Specific Disease rule above** and is being used as an **abnormal-condition class** rather than as one concrete condition instance.  
  - This includes **descriptive, compositional noun phrases** whose **modifiers or complements** signal **abnormality, loss, excess, failure, separation, or similar pathological deviation**, even when they appear inside **descriptive, explanatory, causal, resultative, or parenthetical text**, provided that they denote an **abnormal-condition/process/outcome class** rather than a concrete diagnosed condition.  
  - Do **not** apply this rule to **normal physiological processes**, **normal developmental processes**, **purely mechanistic or causal events**, **procedures/measurements**, **anatomical structures**, **isolated molecular entities/substances**, or **non-pathological descriptive events** that lack inherent disease/abnormal-state semantics.  
  - Do **not** apply this rule when the phrase is mentioned only as a **mechanistic step**, **quantitative/biochemical finding**, **local feature gloss**, or **downstream consequence** of another disease rather than as an independently referred pathological condition or pathological-state class.
- **Do not apply** the preceding **Specific Disease** bullets when the phrase remains an **unanchored general category, phenotype, manifestation, or disease type** mentioned taxonomically or descriptively rather than as a concrete disease entity.  
  - If such a phrase nonetheless denotes an **abnormal-condition class**, annotate it as **Disease Class** under the pathological-state-classes rule above.  
  - If it appears only as an **ordinary feature/symptom item**, **local consequence item**, or **descriptive abnormality item** with no disease-like or pathological-state-class reading in context, do **not** annotate it.
- **Do not apply** the previous bullets when the phrase is only a **mechanistic, laboratory, biochemical, quantitative-readout, neutral-measurement, or transient/experimental functional finding**, including a biochemical feature listed as a **characteristic**, **mechanism**, or **consequence** of another disease, rather than the name of a disease entity or disease class.
- If a disease-denoting phrase would otherwise be **Specific Disease** or **Disease Class** but is being used **attributively** to modify another non-disease concept, annotate it using the **Modifier** category instead.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**

**Example**  
> This report represents the first cases of C7 deficiency associated with infectious complications.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “C7 deficiency” as **Specific Disease** because the deficiency phrase itself names the disorder  
  - “infectious complications” is too general a term to annotate

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.
- This includes **coordinated noun phrases** in which **two or more modifiers, site/location modifiers, parallel premodifiers, and/or complements** share a single head noun that denotes a **lesion, abnormal clinical process, or pathological state**, when the full coordinated span refers to **two or more separate disease mentions** that can be distributively expanded from that shared head.
- This also applies when the shared head is a **non-canonical disease term** and when the coordinated phrase appears inside a **causal, resultative, explanatory, or descriptive clause**, as long as the full span still denotes **multiple parallel pathological-condition mentions**.
- Do **not** use **Composite Mention** when the shared head refers only to a **normal structure**, a **non-pathologic event**, or a **procedure/measurement**, or when the coordination merely lists modifiers/locations without expressing **multiple parallel abnormal-condition mentions**.

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.
- This includes disease terms modifying concepts such as **cases, families, kindreds, patients, population, study, risk, gene, protein, mutation,** or similar non-disease heads.
- Even if a disease-denoting phrase would otherwise qualify as a **Specific Disease** because of **subtype-restricting modifiers** or surrounding **patient-specific clinical context**, use **Modifier** when it is syntactically functioning as a **direct attributive modifier** of a non-disease head.
- If a disease-denoting string (including an acronym) appears near a following biomedical noun but is linked to it through a **reduced predicative clause**—such as a **participial** or **adjectival** relation expressing **causation, association, affectedness,** or another event role—so that the disease term is the **semantic argument** of that predicate, annotate the disease string according to its own category (**Specific Disease** or **Disease Class**) rather than as **Modifier**.
- Do **not** relabel such mentions as **Specific Disease** or **Disease Class** solely because the surrounding sentence discusses diagnosed patients, mutations, associations, or case series; if the disease phrase is syntactically functioning as a **direct modifier** of another concept, use **Modifier**.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

**Example**  
> Germline BRCA1 alterations in a population-based series of ovarian cancer cases.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “ovarian cancer” as **Modifier** in “ovarian cancer cases”  
  - Likewise, annotate “ovarian cancer” as **Modifier** in phrases such as “ovarian cancer population” or “ovarian cancer risk”

**Example**  
> A female with a unilateral variant of Coats disease gave birth to a son affected by Norrie disease.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Norrie disease” as **Specific Disease**, not **Modifier**, because it is the argument of the reduced predicative relation “affected by”

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.
- Prefer the smallest span that still captures the complete disease expression.
- For **Specific Disease** mentions headed by a disease-denoting noun and followed by a **prepositional complement** naming the affected biological substance, gene, protein, or component, include the span from the head noun **through that complement**.
- For abnormal-state mentions that **lack a canonical disease head**, and for **pathological-state/process/result mentions** whose abnormality is expressed compositionally, include the minimal contiguous span that expresses the **pathological condition itself**, together with any modifiers and/or complements necessary to identify that condition.
- Exclude left-side modifiers that only express clinical status such as **degree, severity, zygosity, certainty, or completeness**, unless the modifier is part of a **lexicalized disease name**.
- Include pre-head modifiers when they are part of the lexicalized disease form or when they are necessary to express the locally denoted abnormal condition.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

**Example**  
> In a phrase such as “complete deficiency of complement component C7,” prefer “deficiency of complement component C7” unless “complete deficiency” is itself part of a lexicalized disease name.

**Example**  
> In a phrase such as “unilateral retinal telangiectasis,” prefer the whole phrase when the modifier and anatomical noun together express the locally denoted condition.

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - A microbial or organism-derived modifier is included **only when it is part of an actual disease mention**; a bare organism name remains unannotated.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**  
   - Bare general terms such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” “failure,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” “loss,” “detachment,” “separation,” “accumulation,” etc. were excluded when used alone or as unanchored generic labels.  
   - However, if the **full noun phrase** with its modifiers and/or complements and local context denotes a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class** under the rules above, annotate the full phrase.  
   - This includes common-noun disease phrases whose generic head is instantiated by **subtype-defining narrowing modifiers** or by **patient-specific diagnosis/presentation context**.  
   - The terms “cancer” and “tumor” were retained.

5. **Do not annotate references to biological processes**  
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis.”  
   - Also do **not** annotate normal biological processes/components, isolated genes/proteins/substances, or purely mechanistic descriptions such as reduced expression/activity/absence of a biomolecule when these do **not themselves name** a disease entity or disease class.  
   - Do **not** annotate abnormal experimental, transient, or downstream mechanistic changes when they are presented only as **measurements**, **mechanisms**, **consequences**, **quantitative/biochemical findings**, or **characterizing features** of another disease rather than as independently referred pathological conditions.  
   - If a phrase explicitly asserts an abnormal/pathological state **and functions as a disease mention or pathological-state class**, annotate it according to the rules in **What to Annotate**.

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

---

## Examples

1. **“Insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus”**  
   - **Specific Disease**  
   - Prefer the whole string.

2. **CDH1 mutations predispose to early onset “colorectal cancer.”**  
   - “early onset” may or may not be part of the disease mention, depending on:  
     1. If there is a UMLS concept specifying this as a separate form of disease  
     2. If the annotator believes it should be included

3. **Human “X-linked recessive disorder”**  
   - “X-linked recessive disorder” = **Disease Class**  
   - Exclude “Human”

4. **Her fresh serum lacked complement-mediated bactericidal activity against Neisseria gonorrhoeae.**  
   - Do **not** annotate “complement-mediated bactericidal activity” as a disease mention; it is a biological function/functional finding  
   - Do **not** annotate “Neisseria gonorrhoeae” unless the disease caused by the organism is clearly being referred to

5. **“Huntington disease” (“HD”)**  
   - The long form and the short form constitute two separate mentions.

6. **C7 deficiency**  
   - “This report represents the first cases of C7 deficiency associated with infectious complications.”  
   - “infectious complications” is too general a term; C7 deficiency is a **Specific Disease** because the deficiency phrase itself names the disorder.

7. **“colorectal, endometrial, and ovarian cancers”**  
   - Considered one **Composite Mention** of several Specific Diseases.

8. **WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (“Wilms tumor,” “mesothelioma,” “leukemias,” and “breast cancer”) and nonneoplastic (“glomerulosclerosis”) disease.**  
   - Potential disease mentions include: “neoplastic disease,” “nonneoplastic disease,” “Wilms tumor,” “mesothelioma,” “leukemias,” “breast cancer,” and “glomerulosclerosis.”  
   - “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are **not** annotated because these phrases are interrupted by nested mentions.

9. **Tumorigenesis and cancerogenesis**  
   - These refer to processes, not diseases.

10. **“autosomal recessive disease”**  
    - Refers to a family of diseases that can be passed down genetically → **Disease Class** category.

11. **Large phenotypic variability with “convulsive disorders,” “motor retardation,” and “mental retardation.”**  
    - These are “grey area” terms: if they are being used as abnormal-condition classes rather than as one diagnosed disorder entity, annotate them as **Disease Class**.

12. **Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome (BFLS) is a syndromal X-linked mental retardation.**  
    - “Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome” = **Specific Disease**  
    - “X-linked mental retardation syndrome” = a family of diseases (i.e., **Disease Class**)

13. **Acute meningococcal pericarditis**  
    - Exists as a separate concept in UMLS.  
    - The specific microbial modifier helps make this a **Specific Disease** by narrowing the common-noun pathology head to one concrete disorder entity.

14. **Acute Neisseria infection**  
    - May or may not include “acute” depending on context.  
    - Annotate only when the phrase is being used as the disease mention rather than merely naming the organism.

15. **Classical galactosemia**  
    - Includes “classical” because it corresponds to a particular form of the disease.

16. **Inherited spinocerebellar ataxia**  
    - May or may not include “inherited,” depending on the annotator.

17. **“Adenomatous polyps of the colon and rectum”**  
    - **Composite Mention** because coordinated body-site complements share the abnormal head “polyps” and denote multiple pathologic entities.

18. **Fibroepithelial or epithelial hyperplasias**  
    - **Composite Mention** because coordinated modifiers share the pathologic head “hyperplasias.”

19. **Stage II or stage III colorectal cancer**  
    - **Composite Mention**

20. **“ovarian cancer cases,” “ovarian cancer population,” and “ovarian cancer risk”**  
    - Annotate “ovarian cancer” as **Modifier** because it modifies a non-disease head (“cases,” “population,” “risk”).

21. **“Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein”**  
    - Annotate “Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome” as **Modifier** because it modifies “protein.”

22. **“…resulting in absence of the FMR1 protein.”**  
    - Do **not** annotate “absence of the FMR1 protein” when it is only a mechanistic consequence or functional finding rather than a disease name.

23. **“…results in a deficiency of norrin … within the developing retina.”**  
    - Do **not** annotate “deficiency of norrin” when it is used only as a mechanistic consequence of mutation rather than as the name of a disorder.

24. **“…characterized by … deficient activity of fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase …”**  
    - Do **not** annotate “deficient activity of fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase” when it is listed only as a biochemical feature of another disease rather than as an independently denoted disease entity or disease class.

25. **“complete deficiency of complement component C7”**  
    - Annotate “deficiency of complement component C7” as the disease span unless “complete deficiency” is part of a lexicalized disease name.

26. **Coats' disease of the retina (“unilateral retinal telangiectasis”)**  
    - “Coats' disease” = **Specific Disease**  
    - “unilateral retinal telangiectasis” = **Specific Disease** because the parenthetical phrase is a locally specific named condition/alias, not merely a generic feature label.

27. **Coats disease is characterized by abnormal retinal vascular development … which results in massive intraretinal and subretinal lipid accumulation (“exudative retinal detachment”).**  
    - Do **not** annotate “abnormal retinal vascular development” when it is used only as a characterizing developmental description of Coats disease rather than as an independently referred disease mention, even though it appears in a *characterized by* frame.  
    - Do **not** annotate “retinal telangiectasis” when it appears only as a parenthetical gloss for the local vascular abnormality/feature rather than as the disease mention itself.  
    - Do **not** annotate “exudative retinal detachment” when it is used only as a downstream consequence/feature rather than as an independently referred disease mention or pathological-state class.

28. **A female with a unilateral variant of Coats disease gave birth to a son affected by Norrie disease.**  
    - “Norrie disease” = **Specific Disease** because it is the argument of the reduced predicative relation “affected by,” not a simple **Modifier**.

29. **Most fragile X syndrome patients have expansion of a ( CGG ) ( n ) sequence …**  
    - Annotate “fragile X syndrome” as **Modifier** in “fragile X syndrome patients,” because it directly modifies “patients” with no intervening predicative/event relation.
