# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases.  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization.

**Referential anchoring test.** The label is decided by whether the phrase refers to a concrete, identifiable disorder, not by its surface form.
- Annotate as **Specific Disease** when the phrase refers to a concrete disorder that is being diagnosed, manifested, or ruled out in a specific individual. This is signalled by a governing verb or prepositional frame such as "presenting with/as X," "distinguished from X," "has X," "affected by X," or "variant/form of X." It also applies when the phrase refers to a disorder defined by a specific gene, enzyme, or locus.
- The label applies regardless of surface form. A condition-suffixed head noun, a plural count inflection, or a type-modifier-plus-head structure does not by itself make a phrase a Disease Class, and the label applies even when the phrase has no eponym, acronym, or proper name. The full mention span is treated as one unit, so a type modifier does not turn it into a subtype class (see "classical galactosemia," Example 15).
- Plural number, suffix morphology, or coordination in a list is never sufficient on its own to assign either label.

**Disease Class remains correct** when the phrase is used at the kind level, that is, as an indefinite generic predication (e.g., "is an autosomal recessive disease," "is a syndromal X-linked mental retardation") that does not refer to any particular patient or gene-defined disorder. Members of an unanchored feature list describing a syndrome category are handled under item 8. The predicate frame alone does not decide the label. In "Coats disease is characterized by ...", the subject names a particular disorder and is **Specific Disease**; the generic features in the complement are governed by item 8.

**Precedence.** This rule does not apply to a disease-denoting expression that is an attributive premodifier of a non-disease head noun (e.g., "ovarian cancer cases," "the DTD gene"). Those spans are **Modifier** mentions under item 3, which takes precedence over the referential test and its gene-defined clause. This rule does not alter the boundaries of Modifier or Composite Mention spans.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**

---

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category. A coordinated list of disease terms that functions as one mention is a Composite Mention. Coordination alone never turns a list of symptoms into a Disease Class (see item 8).

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

**Narrower condition (takes precedence over items 1 and 7).** A disease-denoting expression is a **Modifier** when it specifies a non-disease head noun rather than naming the disorder itself. This includes:
- an attributive premodifier of a case, patient, population, sample, study, family/kindred, series, risk, phenotype, gene, or allele noun (e.g., "ovarian cancer cases," "population-based ovarian cancer study," "ovarian cancer risk," "HNPCC kindreds," "ancient SLS genes," "the DTD gene," "G6PD deficient phenotype");
- the complement of a history frame (e.g., "a family history of ovarian cancer").

The Modifier label applies only when the disease expression functions as a modifier. If the disease expression is itself the head of the phrase, or stands as a complete entity (e.g., "Coats telangiectasis"), item 1 or item 7 governs.

**Disease expression inside a nomenclature span.** When a disease-denoting expression (a full-form name whose head is a disease-category noun, or a short form that the document has explicitly defined as a disease) is an attributive premodifier of a non-disease head, label the disease-denoting substring **Modifier**, even when that head sits inside an out-of-schema nomenclature or proper-name construction (e.g., in "the Norrie disease gene (NDP)," "Norrie disease" is a Modifier). Assess that substring independently of the enclosing span, so that the span's out-of-scope status does not suppress the label. Leave non-disease material, such as the head noun or identifier ("gene," "NDP"), unlabelled.
- Do not apply this rule when (a) the disease expression is itself the head of the phrase or stands as a complete entity, in which case the disease-class or specific-disease labels govern; (b) the enclosing name contains no disease-denoting substring, so the span is simply out of scope and remains O; or (c) a short form has no disease definition in the document, so it is not anchored as a disease reference and cannot be promoted to Modifier by position alone.

**Specification versus post-modification.** A disease name that follows a non-disease noun through a "with" or "has" frame is not an attributive premodifier and is annotated as **Specific Disease** under item 1 (e.g., "63 kindreds with SLS"). A disease name that is the disorder of which a form or variant is described (e.g., "the classical form of Coats disease") names the disorder itself and is **Specific Disease**.

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

For enzyme-deficiency states (item 7), the span runs from the state head through the end of its prepositional complement and includes any enzyme name that premodifies the head, because those tokens express the most specific form of the disorder.

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

### 7. Annotate **enzyme-deficiency states** that define a documented disorder
IF a deficiency-type state noun (deficiency, insufficiency, deficit, or a noun that itself denotes reduced activity) is the head of a noun phrase, AND a following prepositional complement introduced by "of" or "in" names a biochemical or gene-product entity (enzyme, protein, or gene symbol) that is the defining molecular phenotype of a disorder described in the document, THEN label the entire span, from the state head through the end of the prepositional complement, as **Specific Disease**. The biochemical name inside the complement must not by itself cause an O label; the negative constraints below are the only exceptions.

- "Defining" means an established characterization of the documented disorder. A deficiency offered as a hypothesized mechanism does not meet this condition.
- The pathological qualifier must be carried by the head noun itself. A neutral head noun (activity, level, expression, function) qualified by a deficiency adjective (e.g., "deficient activity of X," "reduced level of X") is not a state head, and the span remains O.

**Example**  
> “glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency”: the enzyme name premodifies the head, so the full string is one **Specific Disease** mention (Negative Constraint 2).

**Negative constraints.** Do NOT apply this rule when:
1. The deficiency term is an adjectival modifier that does not head a disease noun phrase (e.g., “[enzyme]-deficient”). Label it **Modifier** (item 3).
2. The enzyme or gene name is a premodifier of the disease head (e.g., “[enzyme] deficiency”). The head is already the disease mention, and no prepositional restructuring applies.
3. The head is a bare symptom or sign noun, especially one in a coordinated list. This falls under the symptom-level convention (item 8).
4. The deficiency is reported as a generic biochemical observation and is not tied to a disorder described in the document. This includes in-vitro measurements on individual mutant constructs (e.g., “a severe reduction of FALDH enzyme catalytic activity when expressed in mammalian cells”) and hypothesized mechanisms (e.g., “we suggest that … results in a deficiency of X”). Keep the O label.
5. The state noun is premodified only by a generic word (e.g., “enzyme deficiency”) and its complement is an allele or variant designation (e.g., “in G6PD A-”). This is a variant-level biochemical description. The disorder is annotated through its named mention (e.g., “G6PD deficiency”), and the descriptive phrase remains O.

---

### 8. Annotate **generic manifestations and pathological states** as **Disease Class**
IF a noun phrase’s head denotes a generic clinical manifestation (a sign, symptom, or discrete structural/developmental abnormality) or a generic pathological state (such as a deficiency or dysfunction of a biomolecule expressed through a prepositional complement), with no disease-type head noun and no proper-name disease label, AND the phrase is the complement of a predicate that characterizes a disease or causally links it to a disease-governed subject (e.g., “is characterized by,” “results in,” “is caused by”), THEN tag the full noun phrase, including its premodifiers and prepositional complement, as one **Disease Class** span rather than O.

**Application conditions:**
- **Predicate subject.** The predicate’s subject must be the disease, or a finding already attributed to the disease. A predicate whose subject is an etiological lesion that causes the disease (e.g., “a mutation in the NDP gene, which results in a deficiency of X”) does not license the label.
- **Head-noun test.** Decide from the semantic role of the head noun (manifestation or state versus named entity), not from any particular symptom word or from the predicate verb alone. A neutral head carrying a pathological adjective does not qualify (see item 7).
- **Processes excluded.** Developmental and pathophysiological process descriptions (e.g., “abnormal retinal vascular development,” or accumulation described as a mechanism step) are biological processes under What NOT to Annotate, item 5. Only discrete clinical findings are tagged.
- **Symptom-level convention.** A bare, single-word sign or symptom noun that is a member of a coordinated list (e.g., “ichthyosis,” “spasticity”) remains O. Multi-word phenotype terms, including the grey-area terms “mental retardation” and “motor retardation” (Example 11), are Disease Class.
- **Complement position only.** The noun phrase must be a complement of the characterizing predicate. A noun phrase that is the subject of a predicate (e.g., “WT1 dysfunction is implicated in …”) is not tagged.

**Does NOT apply when:**
- (a) the span is a named disease or uses a disease-type head noun (e.g., “[X] disease/syndrome/disorder/cancer,” or a named “[X] deficiency”), which is **Specific Disease** (item 1 or item 7);
- (b) the span is only a gene, protein, or molecule name with no pathological qualifier, which is O;
- (c) the predicate frame is present but the complement is not a manifestation or pathological state (e.g., a treatment, a population, or a neutral anatomical site), because the frame alone does not license the label;
- (d) the span is a coordinated list of disease terms functioning as a single composite mention, which is **Composite Mention** (item 2);
- (e) the head is a general term (e.g., “complications,” “abnormalities”), which is excluded under What NOT to Annotate, item 4.

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**  
   - Very general terms such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” etc. were excluded when used bare or without a specifying qualifier.  
   - A qualified phrase that names a particular disorder is annotated (e.g., “C7 deficiency,” “Coats disease”) under items 1 and 7. Generic heads such as “complications” or “abnormalities” are not made into mentions by item 8.  
   - However, the terms “cancer” and “tumor” were retained.

5. **Do not annotate references to **biological processes**  
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis.”  
   - This includes pathophysiological and developmental process descriptions (e.g., “abnormal retinal vascular development”), even when they are complements of predicates such as “is characterized by” or “results in” (see item 8).

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

7. **Do not annotate non-disease deficiency descriptions**  
   - Deficiency-type phrases whose head is a neutral noun (e.g., “deficient activity of fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase”), and generic or in-vitro biochemical observations, are not annotated even when an enzyme name is present (see item 7, Negative Constraints 1, 4, and 5).

---

## Precedence Between Overlapping Rules

Where two rules above would label the same span differently, the narrower condition is applied first and the broader rule explicitly excludes it.

1. **Modifier before Specific Disease / Disease Class (item 3 before item 1).** A disease-denoting expression that is an attributive premodifier of a non-disease head, or that specifies a history frame, is a **Modifier** even when its referent is a concrete individual’s disorder. The referential test and its gene-defined clause in item 1 do not override this. A disease expression that is itself the head of a phrase is never a Modifier.

2. **Enzyme-deficiency before generic pathological state (item 7 before item 8).** When the item 7 conditions are satisfied, the span is **Specific Disease** and item 8 does not apply. Where item 7 is not satisfied (e.g., hypothesized mechanism, neutral head, generic or in-vitro observation), item 8 is evaluated on its own conditions. Its predicate-subject and head-noun tests keep such spans O unless every item 8 condition is met.

3. **Item 8 never overrides named disorders, composites, or general-term exclusions.** A named “[X] deficiency” or “[X] disease” remains **Specific Disease**. Coordinated lists of disease terms remain **Composite Mention**. Item 8 and the kind-level rule in item 1 both yield **Disease Class**, so they are consistent.

4. **Adjectival deficiency forms.** An adjectival deficiency form (“[enzyme]-deficient”) is a **Modifier** (item 7, Negative Constraint 1), not a Specific Disease.

**NEW MODERATION PRINCIPLES:**
### 1. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: O (Missed) (6 cases)
- **Principle: Tag Functional-Loss Phrases Anchored to a Molecular Name as SpecificDisease**
  - **IF** a noun phrase's head is a privative or reduced-function state noun (e.g., *deficiency*, *absence*, *lack*, *deficient activity*), **AND** the head is anchored to a gene, enzyme, protein, or component name, either as a premodifier (`[MOLECULE] + head`) or as the complement of a prepositional phrase (`head + of/in + [MOLECULE]`), **AND** the state is predicated of an organism or patient rather than of a reagent, assay, or isolated molecule,
  - **THEN** label the entire phrase, including the state head and any ordinal, numeric, or determiner material inside it, as SpecificDisease. Do not label only the embedded molecular name.
  - **Does NOT apply when:**
    - The head is a mutation, variant, allele, polymorphism, or substitution noun. These denote a genetic cause, so the molecular change stays outside the disease span.
    - The disease name is the premodifier and the molecular name is the head (e.g., "[DISEASE] protein"). These remain Modifier, as the current guideline requires.
    - The state noun takes a reagent, assay, sample, or measurement as its complement (e.g., "activity was measured in serum").
    - The phrase is a bare clinical sign or symptom with no molecular anchor (e.g., "fever"). Treat these as a separate, lower-confidence category pending gold-convention review.
    - The phrase is coordinated or list-structured. Leave boundary decisions to the CompositeMention convention.
  - **Validity check:** The rule labels only named conditions as SpecificDisease, leaves Modifier assignments for protein-headed phrases unchanged, and does not relabel generic DiseaseClass terms that lack a molecular anchor. I checked it against the entity definitions and the guideline as stated in the analysis, because no separate guideline text was provided.

### 2. Gold: DiseaseClass vs LLM: O (Missed) (4 cases)
- **Principle: Abnormal-State Nominals as Disease-Characterizing Complements**
  - **IF** a noun phrase's head denotes a pathological manifestation, symptom, or abnormal state (a sign, an abnormal developmental or functional process, or a deficiency of a biological entity), **AND** that phrase is an argument or coordinate conjunct of a disease-characterizing predicate (a passive "characterized by"-type frame) or a causal predicate ("results in," "due to," "secondary to"), **AND** the governing clause contains a disease mention in the same sentence,
  - **THEN** label the full noun phrase, including its abnormality modifiers, as DiseaseClass, evaluating each conjunct in a coordinated list separately. Do not default to O on the grounds that the phrase is descriptive background.
  - **Does NOT apply when:**
    - The head is a normal biological process, structure, or gene/protein name with no abnormality head.
    - The phrase is a measurement, laboratory value, or treatment/intervention.
    - The disease itself is a named entity or disorder label. That mention keeps its own label (SpecificDisease or DiseaseClass) and is not reassigned by this rule.
    - The predicate is not disease-characterizing or causal, or no disease mention is present in the same sentence.
    - The construction is a "deficient activity of [enzyme]" type. This is a boundary case where the gold standard is inconsistent, so flag it for review rather than applying the rule automatically.

### 3. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: Modifier (Label Mismatch) (2 cases)
- **Attributive Premodifier vs. Closed Disease Mention:** IF a disease-name string is an unbroken, directly attributive modifier that immediately precedes a non-disease head noun, with no intervening preposition, parenthesis, comma, or other punctuation, THEN label it **Modifier**. Otherwise, IF the disease-name string heads its noun phrase, is the complement of a preposition, or is closed by a following parenthesis or acronym, THEN label it **SpecificDisease**, regardless of any adjacent participle, head noun, or repeated use of the string elsewhere in the document.
  - **Negative constraint:** Do NOT assign Modifier when a closing parenthesis, acronym, or punctuation separates the name from a following word, even if that word is a participle or a risk, case, or study head. Do NOT let a preceding preposition, the semantic frame (e.g., family history, risk), or document-level frequency override the syntactic slot. This rule only arbitrates between SpecificDisease and Modifier for a disease string; it does not determine CompositeMention or DiseaseClass boundaries.

### 4. Gold: DiseaseClass vs LLM: Modifier (Label Mismatch) (1 cases)
- **Modifier Is Determined by Pre-Nominal Attributive Position, Not by Lexical Identity**
  - **IF** a disease-denoting span is a specific disease expression (named, subtyped, or qualified by a site or type) that sits directly before the noun it modifies, as an N–N compound or adjectival premodifier, **THEN** label the span **Modifier**.
  - **IF** a disease-denoting span occupies a post-nominal slot, such as the object of a preposition, a clause-final argument, or a standalone head noun phrase, **THEN** do not label it Modifier. Label it **SpecificDisease** if it is a named or subtyped disease, or **DiseaseClass** if it is a generic category noun.
  - **Negative Constraint:** This rule does not apply to:
    - Generic category nouns in pre-nominal position without a specific qualifier. These remain DiseaseClass.
    - Disease spans inside a coordinated or multi-disease expression. Defer to the CompositeMention rules.
    - Prepositional complements whose governing noun expresses a qualifying or relational role (for example, "history," "risk," or "susceptibility"). That semantic role does not make the disease span a Modifier.
    - A Modifier label assigned to the same string in a different position in the same document. That label is not evidence for the current slot.
  - **Generalization:** Assign the label from the syntactic slot first and from the disease's specificity second. The surface string and its labels elsewhere carry no weight.

The Rho-family GTPase , Cdc42 , can regulate the actin cytoskeleton through activation of Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein ( WASP ) family members . Activation relieves an autoinhibitory contact between the GTPase-binding domain and the carboxy-terminal region of WASP proteins .
