# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases.  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**

---

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**  
   - Very general terms such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” etc. were excluded.  
   - However, the terms “cancer” and “tumor” were retained.

5. **Do not annotate references to **biological processes**  
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis.”

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

**NEW MODERATION PRINCIPLES:**
### 1. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: DiseaseClass (Label Mismatch) (5 cases)
- **IF** a disease-denoting phrase refers to a concrete disorder that is being diagnosed, manifested, or ruled out in a specific individual (signaled by a governing verb or prepositional frame such as "presenting with/as X," "distinguished from X," or "has X"), or refers to a disorder defined by a specific gene, enzyme, or locus, **THEN** label it SpecificDisease, regardless of surface form. Surface form includes a condition-suffixed head noun, a plural count inflection, or a type-modifier-plus-head structure, and the label applies even when the phrase has no eponym, acronym, or proper name. Treat the full mention span as one unit, so a type modifier does not turn it into a subtype class.
  - **Negative constraint:** This rule does **NOT** apply when the phrase is a kind-level, umbrella, or phenotype descriptor used generically (for example, a generic "is characterized by" or "is associated with" predication, or an unanchored feature list describing a syndrome category) and does not refer to any particular patient or gene-defined disorder. In those cases, DiseaseClass remains correct. Plural number, suffix morphology, or coordination in a list is never sufficient on its own to assign DiseaseClass or SpecificDisease; the referential-anchoring test must decide.
  - **Validity check:** This is consistent with the DiseaseClass definition (umbrella or kind-level category) and the SpecificDisease definition (an identifiable, concrete disorder). It does not alter the boundaries of Modifier or CompositeMention spans.

### 2. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: O (Missed) (4 cases)
- **Enzyme-Deficiency Complement Principle:** IF a deficiency-type state noun (e.g., a noun meaning deficiency, insufficiency, or reduced activity) is the head of a noun phrase, AND a following prepositional complement introduced by "of" or "in" names a biochemical or gene-product entity (enzyme, protein, or gene symbol) that is the defining molecular phenotype of a disorder described in the document, THEN label the entire span, from the state head through the end of the prepositional complement, as SpecificDisease. The biochemical name inside the complement must not cause an O label.
  - **Negative Constraint:** Do NOT apply this rule when:
    1. The deficiency term is an adjectival modifier that does not head a disease noun phrase (e.g., "[enzyme]-deficient"). Label it Modifier.
    2. The enzyme or gene name is a premodifier of the disease head (e.g., "[enzyme] deficiency"). The head is already the disease mention, and no prepositional restructuring applies.
    3. The head is a bare symptom or sign noun, especially one in a coordinated list. This falls under the separate symptom-level annotation convention.
    4. The deficiency is reported as a generic biochemical observation and is not tied to a disorder described in the document. Keep the O label in this case.

### 3. Gold: Modifier vs LLM: O (Missed) (3 cases)
- **Disease Expression Inside a Nomenclature Span:** IF a disease-denoting expression (a full-form name whose head is a disease-category noun, or a short form the document has explicitly defined as a disease) functions as an attributive premodifier of a non-disease head noun, including when that head sits inside an out-of-schema nomenclature or proper-name construction, THEN label the disease-denoting substring **Modifier**. Assess that substring independently of the enclosing span, so the span's out-of-scope status does not suppress the label. Leave any non-disease material in the span, such as the head noun or identifier, unlabeled.
  - **Negative constraint:** Do NOT apply this rule when (a) the disease-denoting expression is itself the head of the phrase or stands as a complete entity, in which case the disease-class or specific-disease labels govern; (b) the enclosing name contains no disease-denoting substring, so the span is simply out of scope and remains O; or (c) a short form has no disease definition in the document, so it is not anchored as a disease reference and cannot be promoted to Modifier by position alone.

### 4. Gold: DiseaseClass vs LLM: O (Missed) (3 cases)
- **Generic Manifestation or Pathological State as DiseaseClass:** IF a noun phrase's head denotes a generic clinical manifestation (a sign, symptom, or structural/developmental abnormality) or a generic pathological state (such as a deficiency or dysfunction of a biomolecule expressed through a prepositional complement), with no disease-type head noun and no proper-name disease label, AND the phrase is the complement of a predicate that characterizes a disease or causally links it to a disease-governed subject (e.g., "is characterized by," "results in," "is caused by"), THEN tag the full noun phrase, including its premodifiers and prepositional complement, as one DiseaseClass span rather than O.
  - **Does NOT apply when:** (a) the span is a named disease or uses a disease-type head noun (e.g., a "[X] disease/syndrome/disorder/cancer" construction, or a named "[X] deficiency"), which is SpecificDisease; (b) the span is only a gene, protein, or molecule name with no pathological qualifier, which is O; (c) the predicate frame is present but the complement is not a manifestation or pathological state (e.g., a treatment, a population, or a neutral anatomical site), because the frame alone does not license the label; (d) the span is a coordinated list of disease terms functioning as a single composite mention, which is CompositeMention.
  - **Basis for recognition:** Decide from the semantic role of the head noun (manifestation or state versus named entity), not from any specific symptom word, and not from the predicate verb alone.
