# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases.  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**

The sub-rules 1a to 1c below are ordered from narrowest to broadest. Where a span matches more than one, the narrower sub-rule governs, and the broader sub-rule explicitly excludes it.

#### 1a. Deficiency phrases with an enzyme or gene-product complement (narrowest)
IF a state head denoting absent or reduced biological function (for example, “deficiency” or “deficient activity”) is followed by a prepositional complement (“of” or “in”) whose object is an enzyme or gene-product name, AND the phrase names the condition itself (the diagnosis, the patient’s or population’s condition, or the disease under study, supported by causal or etiological predication, inheritance description, or patient or condition framing), THEN annotate the entire span from the state head through the end of the prepositional object as **Specific Disease**. Do not annotate the enzyme name alone.

**Do NOT apply** this rule when:  
1. the state term is a prenominal modifier of an enzyme-bearing compound (a fixed N–N disease name, which keeps its existing tagging);  
2. the state term is an adjective describing an individual’s phenotype (e.g., “G6PD deficient phenotype”), which remains a **Modifier**;  
3. the head is a clinical sign or symptom within a coordinated presentation list with no enzyme-complement construction, which is routed to annotation audit;  
4. the prepositional object is a measured quantity, assay, or specimen rather than a named enzyme or gene product, or the context gives no disorder framing;  
5. the phrase describes a biochemical property of an enzyme variant established experimentally (e.g., reduced catalytic activity of a mutant enzyme expressed in mammalian cells or bacteria) rather than a condition of individuals. It is not annotated, and it is routed to audit if the document reports it as a patient or population condition;  
6. the phrase is one of several manifestations listed for a disease already annotated in the same sentence (e.g., “is characterized by A, B, and deficient activity of Y”). The deficiency is a presenting feature of that disease, not the condition itself, and is routed to audit;  
7. the phrase is a molecular consequence in a causal chain (e.g., “a mutation results in a deficiency of [protein]”) rather than the condition under study.

#### 1b. Referential role (general rule)
A disease mention is labelled by its referential role, not by the superordinate form of its head noun. IF the head is a superordinate pathology or taxonomic category term (for example, a general inflammatory-class form, or a generic infection or deficiency noun, possibly pluralized or modified by a taxon, anatomical, or other qualifier) AND the mention denotes the single specific condition that the document diagnoses, reports as the patient’s diagnosis, or studies (typically as the complement of a diagnostic, presentational, or association construction), THEN annotate it as **Specific Disease**. Qualifiers do not override the head’s referential status. The rule depends only on the head’s morphological category and discourse role, so it applies to unseen category heads.

**Do NOT apply** this sub-rule, because a narrower rule governs, when:  
1. the mention is a prenominal attributive element. Section 3a governs, even when the prenominal disease names the condition the document studies (e.g., “ovarian cancer population” is **Modifier**);  
2. the mention qualifies a relational noun phrase such as “family history of X.” Section 3c governs (**Modifier**);  
3. the mention is an enzyme-complement deficiency phrase. Section 1a governs;  
4. the category term denotes an umbrella spanning several distinct disorders, such as a collective or group-level reference (e.g., “autosomal recessive disease”). Assign **Disease Class**;  
5. the category term is a generic phenotype or symptom descriptor within a presenting-feature enumeration and does not name the case’s diagnosis. It is not annotated automatically, except grey-area labels listed in Example 11, which keep **Disease Class**;  
6. the mention is a biochemical property of an enzyme variant measured experimentally (see Section 1a, item 5).

Where a category-form term sits in a presenting-feature enumeration and could plausibly name the diagnosis, treat it as a boundary case and route it to annotation audit rather than resolving it by form alone.

#### 1c. Non-nosological condition descriptors as Disease Class (broadest)
IF the head of a nominal phrase denotes a clinical sign or symptom, a deficit or state nominalization (a state noun taking a complement introduced by “of” or “in”), or an abnormality adjective plus a pathological-process noun, AND the phrase is predicated of a disease (as a complement in a disease-characterization or causal clause, e.g., “is characterized by,” “results in,” “secondary to,” in which the disease is the subject or referent), THEN annotate the full nominal phrase, including its premodifiers and complement, as **Disease Class**.

**Do NOT apply** this rule when:  
- the head is a named disease (an eponym, an acronym, or a disease, syndrome, or disorder label), including an eponym-bearing name whose head is a sign noun (e.g., “Coats telangiectasis”). These are **Specific Disease**;  
- the condition noun is a prenominal attributive modifier of another entity. This is **Modifier**;  
- the span is only a gene, protein, or molecule name with no pathological-state head;  
- the phrase describes a normal or developmental process, a measurement, or an experimentally characterized property of an enzyme variant, even when the process is qualified as abnormal (e.g., “abnormal retinal vascular development”);  
- the phrase is not predicated of a disease, for example because its subject is a mutation, a gene, or an experimental manipulation;  
- the phrase is a parenthetical or appositive gloss of a disease name, which is not a predication;  
- the phrase is one item in a list of disease manifestations (presenting features) or one step in a multi-step pathophysiological cascade. These are routed to annotation audit rather than tagged automatically, except grey-area condition labels already listed in Example 11 (such as “mental retardation”), which keep **Disease Class**.

**Generalization:** This rule is keyed to the morphosyntactic class of the head and to the clausal or list frame, not to any specific lexical item, so it applies to unseen symptom nouns, deficit nominalizations, and abnormal-process phrases.

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

#### 3a. Prenominal disease elements (precedence rule)
IF a disease name or disease acronym functions as a prenominal (attributive) element that directly precedes a head noun in a noun–noun compound (including adjectival forms such as “G6PD deficient”), THEN annotate the disease span as **Modifier**. Assign the label by syntactic position, not by string identity, so the same acronym can be **Specific Disease** in a parenthetical definition and **Modifier** when prenominal. This rule takes precedence over Section 1b, which excludes prenominal elements. Its statement that a gene or locus compound does not suppress the disease sub-span applies to ad hoc gene references; Section 3b describes the exception for official designations.

- “ovarian cancer population,” “ovarian cancer cases,” “ovarian cancer risk”: “ovarian cancer” is **Modifier**  
- “SLS genes”: “SLS” is **Modifier**  
- “the DTD gene”: “DTD” is **Modifier**  
- “G6PD deficient phenotype”: “G6PD deficient” is **Modifier**

**Do NOT apply** this rule when:  
- (a) the disease string is the rightmost head of its own noun phrase, or is an appositive or parenthetical definition (such as an acronym in parentheses after its full form). In that case it is **Specific Disease**, e.g., “diastrophic dysplasia (DTD)”;  
- (b) the disease string is separated from its head noun by a preposition, verb, or punctuation;  
- (c) the disease string is part of a coordinated structure that belongs to a **Composite Mention**;  
- (d) the prenominal element names a non-disease property, such as an anatomical, chemical, or numeric attribute;  
- (e) the full string is itself a fixed disease name (e.g., the eponym-bearing “Coats telangiectasis”), which is annotated whole as **Specific Disease**.

#### 3b. Official gene or locus designations (narrowed sub-case)
Ad hoc references in which a disease name modifies a generic gene noun (e.g., “SLS genes,” “the DTD gene”) follow Section 3a and are **Modifier**. A disease name that forms part of an official full gene or locus designation (e.g., “breast cancer susceptibility gene 1,” the designation of BRCA1) is a fixed nomenclature token and is **not** annotated as a disease mention. This is a deliberate narrowing of Section 3a for established designations, so that the gene name is not fragmented.

#### 3c. Relational complements
A disease that qualifies a relational noun phrase, such as “a family history of X,” is annotated **Modifier** even though it is not attributive, because it characterizes the relational noun phrase rather than naming a diagnosis. This case is excluded from the referential-role rule in Section 1b.

- “a family history of ovarian cancer”: “ovarian cancer” is **Modifier**.

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**  
   - Very general terms such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” etc. were excluded.  
   - The terms “cancer” and “tumor” are retained when they are part of a specific disease or disease-class mention (e.g., “ovarian cancer,” “Wilms tumor”).  
   - A bare, unqualified “cancer” or “tumor” used generically (e.g., “a family history of cancer”) is a general term and is not annotated. An umbrella **Disease Class** requires a qualified family name (Section 1b).

5. **Do not annotate references to biological processes**  
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis.”  
   - Normal or developmental processes, even when qualified as abnormal (e.g., “abnormal retinal vascular development”), are not disease mentions. Where such a phrase is used to characterize a disease, route it to annotation audit (Section 1c).

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

7. **Do not annotate experimentally measured enzyme properties**  
   - Biochemical properties of enzyme variants characterized in expression systems (e.g., activity or stability of mutant G6PD or FALDH proteins measured in cells or bacteria) are not disease mentions. Route them to annotation audit if the document reports them as a patient or population condition.

8. **Do not annotate presenting features or mechanistic findings**  
   - Signs, symptoms, or biochemical features listed among the manifestations of an annotated disease (e.g., ichthyosis, spasticity), and steps in a pathophysiological cascade, are not annotated, except the grey-area condition labels in Example 11. Route them to annotation audit.

9. **Do not annotate descriptive glosses**  
   - A parenthetical or appositive that restates a disease name with a finding (e.g., “(unilateral retinal telangiectasis)” or “(exudative retinal detachment)”) is not a separate disease mention, unless it is a standard synonym or abbreviation (see Section 6).

## Examples

1. **“Insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus”**  
   - **Specific Disease**  
   - Prefer the whole string.

2. **CDH1 mutations predispose to early onset “colorectal cancer.”**  
   - “early onset” may or may not be part of the disease mention, depending on:  
     1. If there is a UMLS concept specifying this as a separate form of disease  
     2. If the annotator believes it should be included

3. **Human “X-linked recessive disorder”**

4. **Her fresh serum lacked complement-mediated bactericidal activity against Neisseria gonorrhoeae.**

5. **“Huntington disease” (“HD”)**  
   - The long form and the short form constitute two separate mentions.

6. **C7 deficiency**  
   - “This report represents the first cases of C7 deficiency associated with infectious complications.”  
   - “infectious complications” is too general a term; C7 deficiency is a **Specific Disease**.

7. **“colorectal, endometrial, and ovarian cancers”**  
   - Considered one **Composite Mention** of several Specific Diseases.

8. **WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (“Wilms tumor,” “mesothelioma,” “leukemias,” and “breast cancer”) and nonneoplastic (“glomerulosclerosis”) disease.**  
   - Potential disease mentions include: “neoplastic disease,” “nonneoplastic disease,” “Wilms tumor,” “mesothelioma,” “leukemias,” “breast cancer,” and “glomerulosclerosis.”  
   - “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are **not** annotated because these phrases are interrupted by nested mentions.

9. **Tumorigenesis and cancerogenesis**  
   - These refer to processes, not diseases.

10. **“autosomal recessive disease”**  
    - Refers to a family of diseases that can be passed down genetically → **Disease Class** category.

11. **Large phenotypic variability with “convulsive disorders,” “motor retardation,” and “mental retardation.”**  
    - These are “grey area” terms: they may not correspond to a single **Specific Disease**, so they are annotated as **Disease Class** category if appropriate.

12. **Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome (BFLS) is a syndromal X-linked mental retardation.**  
    - “Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome” = **Specific Disease**  
    - “X-linked mental retardation syndrome” = a family of diseases (i.e., **Disease Class**)

13. **Acute meningococcal pericarditis**  
    - Exists as a separate concept in UMLS.

14. **Acute Neisseria infection**  
    - May or may not include “acute” depending on context.

15. **Classical galactosemia**  
    - Includes “classical” because it corresponds to a particular form of the disease.

16. **Inherited spinocerebellar ataxia**  
    - May or may not include “inherited,” depending on the annotator.

17. **“Adenomatous polyps of the colon and rectum”**  
    - **Composite Mention**

18. **Fibroepithelial or epithelial hyperplasias**  
    - **Composite Mention**

19. **Stage II or stage III colorectal cancer**  
    - **Composite Mention**

20. **“ovarian cancer population,” “ovarian cancer cases,” “ovarian cancer risk”**  
    - “ovarian cancer” is **Modifier** (prenominal, Section 3a). This takes precedence over the referential-role rule even though the document studies this condition.

21. **“a family history of ovarian cancer”**  
    - “ovarian cancer” is **Modifier** (relational complement, Section 3c).

22. **“breast cancer susceptibility gene 1 (BRCA1)”**  
    - Official gene designation. “breast cancer” is not annotated (Section 3b).

23. **“diastrophic dysplasia (DTD)” … “the DTD gene”**  
    - The parenthetical “DTD” is **Specific Disease** (Section 3a(a)). The prenominal “DTD” in “the DTD gene” is **Modifier** (Section 3a). The same acronym receives different labels by syntactic position.

24. **“Sjogren-Larsson syndrome (SLS) is an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by ichthyosis, mental retardation, spasticity, and deficient activity of fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase (FALDH)”**  
    - The disease remains **Specific Disease**. “mental retardation” keeps **Disease Class** (grey-area label, Example 11). “ichthyosis,” “spasticity,” and “deficient activity of FALDH” are presenting features of an annotated disease and are routed to audit, not annotated (Section 1a, item 6; What NOT 8).

25. **“reduction of FALDH enzyme catalytic activity when expressed in mammalian cells”; “enzyme deficiency in G6PD A-” in a bacterial expression study**  
    - Experimentally measured properties of enzyme variants. Not annotated; routed to audit (Section 1a, item 5; What NOT 7).

26. **“Coats telangiectasis is secondary to somatic mutation in the NDP gene which results in a deficiency of norrin”**  
    - “Coats telangiectasis” is **Specific Disease**, annotated whole as a fixed eponym-bearing name (Section 1c exclusion; Section 3a(e)).  
    - “deficiency of norrin” is a molecular consequence of a mutation rather than the condition under study. Not annotated (Section 1a, item 7; Section 1c).

27. **“Coats disease is characterized by abnormal retinal vascular development (so-called retinal telangiectasis) which results in massive intraretinal and subretinal lipid accumulation (exudative retinal detachment).”**  
    - “Coats disease” is **Specific Disease**.  
    - “abnormal retinal vascular development” is a developmental process (Section 1c). “lipid accumulation” is a step in a cascade. Both are routed to audit, not annotated. The parenthetical glosses are not annotated (What NOT 9).

28. **“a family history of cancer”**  
    - Bare, unqualified “cancer” is a general term and is not annotated (What NOT 4).

29. **“Wilms tumor”**  
    - “Wilms tumor” is **Specific Disease**. “cancer” and “tumor” are retained as parts of a specific disease mention (What NOT 4).

## Boundary Cases and Audit Routing

Some mentions match more than one rule, or describe a feature of a disease rather than a disease identity. Resolve them in the following precedence order, narrowest condition first. Each broader rule explicitly excludes the narrower rules above it.

1. **Official gene or locus designations** (Section 3b): not annotated.
2. **Prenominal disease elements** (Section 3a): **Modifier**.
3. **Relational complements** such as “family history of X” (Section 3c): **Modifier**.
4. **Enzyme-complement deficiency phrases** (Section 1a): **Specific Disease**, unless an exclusion in Section 1a applies.
5. **Non-nosological condition descriptors** (Section 1c): **Disease Class**.
6. **Referential role** (Section 1b): **Specific Disease** or **Disease Class**, according to the head’s referential status.

**Route to annotation audit** (not annotated until reviewed):
- signs, symptoms, or biochemical features listed among the manifestations of an annotated disease, except grey-area condition labels listed in Example 11;
- steps of a pathophysiological cascade, and developmental or normal-process phrases used to characterize a disease;
- enzyme deficiency phrases whose enzyme variant is characterized only experimentally;
- category-form terms in a presenting-feature enumeration that could plausibly name the diagnosis.
