# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases.  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**

---

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**  
   - Very general terms such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” etc. were excluded.  
   - However, the terms “cancer” and “tumor” were retained.

5. **Do not annotate references to **biological processes**  
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis.”

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

**NEW MODERATION PRINCIPLES:**
### 1. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: DiseaseClass (Label Mismatch) (6 cases)
- **Principle: Label a disease mention by its referential role, not by the superordinate form of its head noun.**
  - **IF** the head of a disease mention is a superordinate pathology or taxonomic category term (for example, a general inflammatory-class form, a generic infection or deficiency noun, possibly pluralized or modified by a taxon, anatomical, or other qualifier) **AND** the mention denotes the single specific condition that the document diagnoses, reports as the patient's diagnosis, or studies (typically as the complement of a diagnostic, presentational, or association construction), **THEN** assign SpecificDisease to the mention. Qualifiers do not override the head's referential status.
  - **Negative constraint:** Do NOT apply this rule when the category term (a) denotes an umbrella spanning several distinct disorders, such as a collective or group-level reference, in which case assign DiseaseClass, or (b) functions as a generic phenotype or symptom descriptor within a feature enumeration and does not name the case's diagnosis, in which case assign DiseaseClass. Where a category-form term sits in a presenting-feature enumeration and could plausibly name the diagnosis, treat it as a boundary case and flag it for annotation-convention review rather than resolving it by form alone.
  - **Generalization:** The rule depends only on the head's morphological category and the mention's discourse role, not on any particular lexical item, so it applies to unseen disease-category heads.

### 2. Gold: Modifier vs LLM: O (Missed) (4 cases)
- **IF** a disease name or disease acronym functions as a prenominal (attributive) element that directly precedes a head noun in a noun–noun compound, **THEN** label the disease span **Modifier**, even when the full compound functions as a gene, protein, or locus name. Do not let the nomenclatural status of the compound suppress the disease sub-span to O. Assign the label by syntactic position, not by string identity, so the same acronym can be SpecificDisease in a parenthetical and Modifier when prenominal. **Do NOT apply** this rule when (a) the disease string is the rightmost head of its own noun phrase, or is an appositive or parenthetical definition (such as an acronym in parentheses after its full form), in which case label it **SpecificDisease**; (b) the disease string is separated from its head noun by a preposition, verb, or punctuation; (c) the disease string is part of a coordinated structure that belongs to a **CompositeMention**; or (d) the prenominal element names a non-disease property, such as an anatomical, chemical, or numeric attribute.

### 3. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: O (Missed) (4 cases)
- **Postnominal Enzyme Complement Span Rule:** IF a state-denoting head noun of absent or reduced biological function (e.g., a deficiency or deficient-activity phrase) is followed by a prepositional complement ("of" or "in") whose object is an enzyme or gene-product name, AND the surrounding context supports a disorder reading (causal or etiological predication, inheritance description, or patient or condition framing), THEN tag the entire span from the state head through the end of the prepositional object as SpecificDisease, and do not tag the enzyme name alone.
  - **Negative constraint:** Do NOT apply this rule when (1) the state term is a prenominal modifier of the enzyme-bearing compound (a fixed N-N disease name, which keeps its existing tagging); (2) the state term is an adjective describing an individual's phenotype, which remains a Modifier; (3) the head is a clinical sign or symptom within a coordinated presentation list with no enzyme-complement construction, which should be routed to annotation audit rather than tagged by this rule; or (4) the prepositional object is a measured quantity, assay, or specimen rather than a named enzyme or gene product, or the context gives no disorder framing.

### 4. Gold: DiseaseClass vs LLM: O (Missed) (3 cases)
- **Principle: Tag non-nosological condition descriptors as DiseaseClass when they characterize a disease.**
  - **IF** the head noun of a nominal phrase denotes a clinical sign or symptom, a deficit or state nominalization (a state noun taking a complement introduced by a preposition such as "of"), or an abnormality adjective plus a pathological process noun, **AND** the phrase is predicated of a disease, either as a complement in a disease-characterization or causal clause (e.g., "is characterized by," "results in," "secondary to") or as an item in a coordinated list of disease manifestations,
  - **THEN** tag the full nominal phrase, including its premodifiers and prepositional complement, as **DiseaseClass**.
  - **Negative constraint:** Do **not** apply this rule when:
    - the head is a named disease (an eponym, an acronym, or a disease, syndrome, or disorder label), which is **SpecificDisease**;
    - the condition noun is a pre-nominal attributive modifier of another entity, which is **Modifier**;
    - the span is only a gene, protein, or molecule name with no pathological-state head;
    - the phrase describes a normal process or measurement, or is not predicated of a disease.
  - **Generalization:** The rule is keyed to the morphosyntactic class of the head and to the clausal or list frame, not to any specific lexical item, so it applies to unseen symptom nouns, deficit nominalizations, and abnormal-process phrases.
