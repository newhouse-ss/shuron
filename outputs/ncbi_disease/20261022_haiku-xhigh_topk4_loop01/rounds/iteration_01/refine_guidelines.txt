# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases.  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**

---

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**  
   - Very general terms such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” etc. were excluded.  
   - However, the terms “cancer” and “tumor” were retained.

5. **Do not annotate references to **biological processes**  
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis.”

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

**NEW MODERATION PRINCIPLES:**
### 1. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: O (Missed) (6 cases)
- **Privative State-Nominal Naming a Gene-Product Condition:** IF a noun phrase combines a privative or state head (a noun denoting absence, lack, or reduced amount or activity of a function) with a prepositional or genitive complement naming a gene product (a full enzyme or protein name, or its symbol), AND the phrase functions as the name of a condition, meaning it is the argument of a causal or characterizing predicate or identifies a patient's or population's genotype-level state, THEN label the entire noun phrase, including the gene-product name, as **SpecificDisease**. Do not split off the head noun or the gene-product name alone.
  - **Negative Constraint (O or other labels):** Do NOT apply this rule when:
    - The privative element is an adjective modifying a phenotype, patient, or sample (e.g., "deficient phenotype"). Label this **Modifier**.
    - The phrase reports a mechanistic, biochemical, or experimental quantity with no disorder framing, causal predicate, or patient/population reference. Leave it **O**.
    - The phrase already contains a disease-class head noun or an eponym. The standard disease-nominal rules govern these, and this rule does not add a new span.
    - The phrase is a coordinated composite of several conditions. Defer to **CompositeMention** conventions.

### 2. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: DiseaseClass (Label Mismatch) (6 cases)
- **Principle: Referential scope takes precedence over head-noun genericity.**
  - **IF** a mention's head noun is a generic disease-category term, regardless of its morphology, number, or inflection, **AND** the mention refers to one individuated condition that the text is about, because a narrowing modifier (etiologic, genetic, organism, or anatomical-site) anchors it to a single entity, or because it is framed as the patient's or case's diagnosis or clinical event (e.g., through a participial or "presenting with/as" construction),
  - **THEN** label it SpecificDisease, not DiseaseClass.
  - **Negative constraint:** Do NOT apply this rule when the mention denotes a heterogeneous umbrella spanning multiple distinct etiologies, signaled by quantification, enumeration, or a set-denoting construction (e.g., "a variety of," or a list introduced by an exemplifying phrase). Also do NOT apply it when the noun is used in a generic or kind sense about diseases in general rather than a particular case, or when the mention is a non-disease finding.

### 3. Gold: Modifier vs LLM: O (Missed) (4 cases)
- **Disease Premodifier of a Gene/Protein Head:** IF a disease name, disease acronym, or eponym is the leftmost element of a noun phrase whose head noun denotes a gene, protein, locus, or similar molecular unit (with or without a numeric or alphanumeric suffix), THEN label only the disease tokens as Modifier. Label the head noun, any numeric or alphanumeric suffix, and any bracketed gene symbol as O. The compound's resemblance to a gene or protein name must not cause the disease tokens to be labeled O.
  - **Negative constraint:** Do NOT apply this rule when the disease term is not a premodifier of a gene/protein head. This includes a standalone disease name with no following molecular head (e.g., "Coats disease"), a disease term that heads the phrase or modifies a clinical or epidemiological head (e.g., "ovarian cancer cases"), a disease term that is the complement of a preposition, and spans with no disease word (e.g., a bare gene symbol or a gene name built from non-disease words). In these cases, existing disease-mention guidelines continue to govern.

### 4. Gold: DiseaseClass vs LLM: O (Missed) (3 cases)
- **Principle (Generic Pathological-State Descriptors as DiseaseClass):**
  - **IF** a span's head is a generic, non-named noun phrase denoting an abnormal state of the patient (a symptom or phenotypic-abnormality noun, a pathological-process nominal optionally preceded by an abnormality qualifier, or a deficiency-state nominal), **AND** that span functions as a predicate complement, an item in a coordinated feature list, or a consequence/manifestation argument of a clause whose anchor is a named disease or disorder (for example, after a characterization predicate, a causal or result connective, or a "secondary to" construction), **THEN** label the span as **DiseaseClass**, including the qualifier that modifies the state-denoting head, even when the span has no disease-name morphology (no "disease," "syndrome," "-osis," or "-opathy").
  - **Deficiency phrases:** label only the state-denoting span. Do not label the molecular, cellular, or genetic entity that is the argument of the deficiency nominal.
  - **Negative constraints:** Do **not** apply this rule when:
    - the span is itself a named disorder or a SpecificDisease-type mention;
    - the span denotes a normal physiological process, normal anatomical structure, or gene, protein, or other biological product;
    - the span denotes an etiological agent, such as a mutation, variant, or exposure, rather than a state of the patient;
    - the descriptor appears only as background mechanism with no stated link to a named disease within the same clause or sentence;
    - the qualifier is a standalone modifier that does not form part of the state-denoting noun phrase.
  - **Generalization:** The trigger is the syntactic role (predicate complement, coordinated list item, or consequence argument of a disease-anchored clause) combined with the semantic class of the head noun (abnormal patient state). Lexical disease-name cues are not required. Do not rely on any specific word list.
