# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases (for example, an inheritance-pattern category such as “autosomal recessive disease”).  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**

#### 1.1 Referential scope takes precedence over head-noun genericity
A mention whose head noun is a generic disease-category term (e.g., “disease,” “disorder,” “syndrome,” “condition”), regardless of morphology, number, or inflection, is annotated as **Specific Disease** rather than **Disease Class** when it refers to one individuated condition that the text is about. This applies when either:
- a narrowing modifier anchors it to a single entity: an etiologic agent, a specific gene or locus, a specific organism, or a specific anatomical site; or
- it is framed as the patient’s or case’s diagnosis or clinical event (e.g., through a participial or “presenting with / diagnosed with” construction).

A modifier that names a category shared by many diseases does not anchor the mention. An inheritance pattern such as “autosomal recessive” or “X-linked” narrows the class but does not pick out one condition. “Autosomal recessive disease” in the example above therefore remains **Disease Class**.

**Do not apply this rule** when:
- the mention denotes a heterogeneous umbrella spanning multiple distinct etiologies, signaled by quantification, enumeration, or a set-denoting construction (e.g., “a variety of …”, or a list introduced by an exemplifying phrase such as “such as”);
- the mention is one item in a coordinated enumeration of conditions or features (e.g., “characterized by A, B, and C”), because enumeration signals a set rather than one individuated condition;
- the noun is used in a generic or kind sense about diseases in general rather than about a particular case;
- the mention is a non-disease finding.

In these cases the standard Disease Class rules apply, or the mention is left unannotated under “What NOT to Annotate,” item 4.

This rule decides between Specific Disease and Disease Class only. It does not re-label spans already annotated as **Modifier** (Section 3).

- **Individuated (Specific Disease):** “The proband was diagnosed with an X-linked disorder.” → “X-linked disorder” as **Specific Disease** (patient-diagnosis framing).
- **Category (Disease Class):** “X-linked mental retardation syndrome” used as a family of conditions → **Disease Class** (Example 12).

#### 1.2 Privative phrases that name a gene-product condition
When a noun phrase combines a privative or state head (a noun denoting absence, lack, or reduced amount or activity of a function, such as “deficiency,” “absence,” “lack,” or “deficient activity”) with a prepositional or genitive complement naming a gene product (a full enzyme or protein name, or its symbol), and the phrase functions as the name of a condition, label the **entire noun phrase**, including the gene-product name, as **Specific Disease**. The phrase functions as the name of a condition when it is the argument of a causal or characterizing predicate (e.g., “results in,” “caused by,” “characterized by”) or when it identifies a patient’s or population’s genotype-level state. Do not split off the head noun or the gene-product name alone.

- **Example:** “Patients with deficiency of adenosine deaminase presented with recurrent infections.” → “deficiency of adenosine deaminase” as **Specific Disease** (whole noun phrase).

**Do not apply this rule** when:
- The privative element is an adjective modifying a phenotype, patient, or sample (e.g., “G6PD deficient phenotype”). Label this **Modifier** (Section 3.2).
- The phrase reports a mechanistic, biochemical, or experimental quantity with no disorder framing, causal predicate, or patient/population reference. Leave it unannotated (“What NOT to Annotate,” item 7).
- The phrase already contains a disease-class head noun or an eponym. The standard disease-nominal rules govern these, and this rule does not add a new span.
- The phrase is a coordinated composite of several conditions. Defer to **Composite Mention** conventions.
- The gene-product name is a premodifier rather than a complement (e.g., “glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency”). The standard disease-nominal rules already annotate the full string, and this rule adds nothing.

#### 1.3 Generic pathological-state descriptors as Disease Class
A span is annotated as **Disease Class**, including any qualifier that modifies the state-denoting head, when:
1. its head is a generic, non-named noun phrase denoting an abnormal state of the patient: a symptom or phenotypic-abnormality noun, a pathological-state nominal describing a patient-level finding (optionally preceded by an abnormality qualifier), or a deficiency-state nominal; **and**
2. relative to a named disease or disorder in the same clause or sentence, it functions as a predicate complement (e.g., after “is characterized by”), an item in a coordinated feature list, or a consequence or manifestation argument (e.g., after a causal or result connective, or a “secondary to” construction).

Lexical disease cues such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “-osis,” or “-opathy” are not required. The trigger is the syntactic role combined with the semantic class of the head. No word list is used.

- **Deficiency phrases:** label only the state-denoting span. Do not label the molecular, cellular, or genetic entity that is the argument of the deficiency nominal. A deficiency of a named gene product is governed by Section 1.2 instead.
- **Process nominals:** tissue-level, cellular, or developmental process nominals (e.g., “development,” “accumulation,” “tumorigenesis”) describe biological processes and are excluded by “What NOT to Annotate,” item 5, even when preceded by an abnormality qualifier.

**Do not apply this rule** when:
- the span is itself a named disorder or a Specific Disease-type mention, including eponym- or site-named conditions such as “Coats telangiectasis.” Named disorders follow Sections 1 and 1.1. An established disorder term such as “mental retardation” that appears in a feature list remains **Disease Class** under Example 11;
- the span denotes a normal physiological process, a normal anatomical structure, or a gene, protein, or other biological product;
- the span denotes an etiological agent (a mutation, variant, or exposure) rather than a state of the patient;
- the descriptor appears only as background mechanism with no stated link to a named disease within the same clause or sentence;
- the qualifier is a standalone modifier that does not form part of the state-denoting noun phrase. If the state noun is privatively modified as a phenotype or patient descriptor (e.g., “deficient phenotype”), Section 3.2 governs;
- the head is an unqualified or unspecified umbrella term (e.g., “complications,” “abnormalities,” “problems,” bare “deficiency”). These remain excluded by “What NOT to Annotate,” item 4, even in trigger positions. For example, in “C7 deficiency associated with infectious complications,” only “C7 deficiency” is annotated.

- **Example (illustrative):** “Disorder X is characterized by seizures, hypotonia, and spasticity.” → “Disorder X” as **Specific Disease**; “seizures,” “hypotonia,” and “spasticity” as **Disease Class**.
- **Example:** “Coats disease is characterized by abnormal retinal vascular development, which results in massive intraretinal and subretinal lipid accumulation.” → “Coats disease” as **Specific Disease**. The developmental and accumulation phrases are biological-process references and are not annotated.

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

#### 3.1 Disease premodifiers of gene or protein heads
If a disease name, disease acronym, or eponym is the leftmost element of a noun phrase whose head noun denotes a gene, protein, locus, or similar molecular unit (with or without a numeric or alphanumeric suffix), annotate only the disease tokens as **Modifier**. The head noun, any numeric or alphanumeric suffix, and any bracketed gene symbol are left unannotated. The compound’s resemblance to a gene or protein name does not change this label.

- **Examples**  
  - “ancient SLS genes” → “SLS” as **Modifier**  
  - “breast cancer susceptibility gene 1 (BRCA1)” → “breast cancer” as **Modifier**; “gene 1 (BRCA1)” unannotated  
  - “the DTD gene” → “DTD” as **Modifier**

**Do not apply this rule** when the disease term is not a premodifier of a gene or protein head:
- a standalone disease name with no following molecular head (e.g., “Coats disease”);
- a disease term that heads the phrase or modifies a clinical or epidemiological head (e.g., “ovarian cancer cases,” “ovarian cancer population”). Existing Modifier rules govern these;
- a disease term that is the complement of a preposition;
- spans in which the leftmost element is a gene or enzyme symbol rather than a disease name (e.g., “G6PD A- genes”), or a gene name built from non-disease words.

#### 3.2 Privative adjectives modifying a phenotype, patient, or sample
When a privative adjective modifies a phenotype, patient, or sample, the disease-containing span is a **Modifier** and is not a Specific Disease. This is the negative case of Section 1.2.

- **Example:** “G6PD deficient” in “the G6PD deficient phenotype” → **Modifier**.

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

For privative phrases under Section 1.2, the span is the whole noun phrase including the gene-product name, because splitting it loses the condition name. For gene premodifiers under Section 3.1, only the disease tokens are the span.

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

## Rule Precedence for Overlapping Principles

The principles above were derived independently and may overlap. The narrower condition is applied first, and each broader rule explicitly excludes the narrower cases. Where two rules would label the same span differently, apply them in this order:

1. **Composite Mention** (coordinated conditions) overrides Sections 1.1 and 1.2.
2. **Section 1.2** (privative gene-product condition) is applied before **Section 1.3** (generic deficiency-state descriptors). Section 1.3 excludes gene-product complements.
3. **Section 3.1** (disease premodifier of a gene or protein head) determines the label for the disease tokens: **Modifier**, not **Specific Disease**.
4. **Section 1.1** (individuated generic heads) overrides the bare-term exclusion in “What NOT to Annotate,” item 4, only when the mention is individuated. Umbrella and enumerated uses remain excluded.
5. **“What NOT to Annotate,” item 5** (biological-process nominals) overrides the pathological-process trigger in Section 1.3.
6. **Named disorders** are governed by Sections 1 and 1.1, not by Section 1.3.

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**  
   - Very general terms such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” etc. are excluded when used bare or as unspecified umbrella placeholders.  
   - These terms are annotated when they refer to one individuated condition (Section 1.1), or when they are part of a specific name (e.g., “C7 deficiency,” Section 1.2).  
   - However, the terms “cancer” and “tumor” were retained.

5. **Do not annotate references to biological processes**  
   - Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis” are not annotated.  
   - This extends to developmental, tissue-level, or cellular process nominals (e.g., “development,” “accumulation”), including when preceded by an abnormality qualifier, because they describe mechanisms rather than states of the patient (Section 1.3).

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

7. **Do not annotate mechanistic, biochemical, or experimental quantities**  
   - Measured properties of a gene product or experimental system, such as specific activity, enzyme yield, thermal stability, or expression level, are not annotated.  
   - The exception is a phrase that names a condition under Section 1.2 (e.g., a privative gene-product phrase with disorder framing or a patient/population reference).

**NEW MODERATION PRINCIPLES:**
### 1. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: DiseaseClass (Label Mismatch) (5 cases)
- **Referent Over Form:** IF a common-noun phrase (lowercase, non-eponymous, with *-itis* or symptom-like morphology) names the individual patient's own diagnosed or presenting condition, meaning it is the argument of a case-level predicate (presentation, diagnosis, or finding frames such as "presenting with/as," "found to have," "diagnosed with," or "distinguished from other causes of") or is narrowed by a subtype qualifier, THEN label it SpecificDisease. IF the same kind of phrase instead describes a disease type or grouping, such as a phenotypic attribute in a "characterized by" list, a "feature of" construction, or a parenthetical gloss on a named disease, THEN label it DiseaseClass.
  - **Negative Constraint:** Do not assign either label from surface morphology, lowercase form, or list position alone. Coordination in a clinical list does not by itself make a term a category. The rule does not reclassify eponymous or acronymic disease names, which remain SpecificDisease. It does not govern the boundaries of Modifier tokens (standalone qualifiers such as *chronic* or *cutaneous* when annotated separately) or CompositeMention spans, which follow their own definitions. Bare sign or symptom nouns with no diagnostic status are a boundary case: flag them for guideline review rather than forcing either label.

### 2. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: SpecificDisease (Span Boundary Error) (4 cases)
- **Post-nominal molecular complements of state nouns are not disease spans:** IF a noun phrase has a privative state head (a noun denoting absence or reduction, such as "deficiency") followed by an of/in prepositional phrase whose object is a molecular entity (a gene, protein, enzyme, or complement component) that describes the mechanism, THEN do not extend a SpecificDisease span across the preposition into that entity. Leave the head-plus-prepositional-phrase string untagged as SpecificDisease, and tag SpecificDisease only on a separately lexicalized disorder name, keeping the span limited to that name.
  - **Does NOT apply when** the molecular term is a premodifier inside a compact, lexicalized disorder name, where the entity precedes the head and no preposition intervenes (e.g., "G6PD deficiency" remains SpecificDisease as a whole).
  - **Does NOT apply when** the construction is adjectival or participial (e.g., "X-deficient"), which is governed by the Modifier definition rather than SpecificDisease.
  - **Does NOT apply when** the prepositional object names an organism-level condition or a disorder rather than a molecular entity, since that is an independent disease mention.

### 3. Gold: DiseaseClass vs LLM: SpecificDisease (Label Mismatch) (2 cases)
- **Named-Entity vs. Category Reference Test:** IF a disease-denoting mention is either (a) a bare or unqualified category head noun, or (b) a head noun followed by a free prepositional complement (an "X of Y" frame) whose complement names a substance, gene product, or measured variable rather than a recognized condition, THEN assign DiseaseClass, because the mention refers to a generic category, phenotype, or pathophysiological state. Assign SpecificDisease only when the mention individuates one recognized condition through an eponym, an acronym of a named disease, or a fixed nomenclature compound in which the modifier is attached directly to the head rather than linked by a free preposition.
  - **Does NOT apply** when the category noun is an established component of a proper name or fixed term (e.g., "[Eponym] syndrome," "[Acronym] disease"). These remain SpecificDisease regardless of how generic the head noun looks in isolation.
  - **Does NOT apply** to mentions that lack a disease-denoting head and are not classified as disease entities. Those are governed by the Modifier and CompositeMention definitions, not by this rule.
  - **Does NOT override** the SpecificDisease definition for fixed compounds that name a specific enzyme or gene deficiency and are recognized as a single disease. The distinction depends on whether the modifier is a fixed nomenclature element or a free complement, not on the presence of the head noun itself.

### 4. Gold: O vs LLM: SpecificDisease (Added) (2 cases)
- **Principle (Name-versus-Reference Gate):** IF a candidate span's disease meaning comes only from a generic or anaphoric head, meaning a determiner or demonstrative plus a disease-semantic noun ("this condition," "such disorder") that points back to an antecedent, or from a descriptive finding, meaning a state or absence noun with a prepositional complement that reports an observed or measured entity ("absence of [molecule]," "lack of [component]"), THEN do not label the span SpecificDisease. Leave it O, because the disease is named elsewhere or the span reports a finding rather than naming a disease.
  - **Test:** Can the span be read as a self-contained disease name without resolving a pronoun or interpreting a clinical finding? If not, it is not SpecificDisease.
  - **Negative Constraint:** This rule does NOT apply when the span is a lexicalized disease name, even if its head noun is generic. This includes eponyms, disease acronyms, and fixed compound labels such as "[X] deficiency," "[X] syndrome," or "[X] cancer." It also does not remove labels assigned under other definitions: a span that independently meets the DiseaseClass or CompositeMention definition keeps that label. The rule only blocks SpecificDisease.
  - **Generalization:** The rule is stated in terms of grammatical role and reference status: anaphoric determiners and demonstratives, and state-noun constructions with prepositional complements, versus proper-noun, acronym, and fixed-compound names. It does not depend on any particular disease term, so it applies to unseen spans that share these structures.

Here we report the autoinhibited structure of the GTPase-binding domain of WASP , which can be induced by the C-terminal region or by organic co-solvents . In the autoinhibited complex , intramolecular interactions with the GTPase-binding domain occlude residues of the C terminus that regulate the Arp2 / 3 actin-nucleating complex . Binding of Cdc42 to the GTPase-binding domain causes a dramatic conformational change , resulting in disruption of the hydrophobic core and release of the C terminus , enabling its interaction with the actin regulatory machinery . These data show that intrinsically unstructured peptides such as the GTPase-binding domain of WASP can be induced into distinct structural and functional states depending on context . .

TEXT: Both mutations in G6PD A- are necessary to produce the G6PD deficient phenotype.
The high prevalence of glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase ( G6PD ) deficiency in African populations is due almost entirely to the enzyme variant A- , which differs from the wild-type G6PD B by two amino acid replacements , 68 Val-- > Met and 126 Asn-- > Asp . The non-deficient polymorphic variant G6PD A contains only the mutation 126 Asn-- > Asp . The frequencies of the G6PD A and of the G6PD A- genes in parts of Africa are both about 0 . 2 . The 68 Val-- > Met mutation has not been found in a B background . This could be because the 68 Val-- > Met mutation happened to arise in an A gene in the first instance , or because the 68 Val-- > Met mutation alone is not sufficient to cause G6PD deficiency . We have approached this question by producing G6PD B , A , A- , and G6PD 68 Val-- > Met in a bacterial expression system and analysing their biochemical properties . With each single mutation we found a slight decrease in both the specific activity and the yield of enzyme when compared to G6PD B . When both mutations were introduced together , there was a roughly additive effect on specific activity , but a much more drastic effect on enzyme yield ( 4 % of normal ) . This synergistic effect was also demonstrated on thermal stability , especially at low NADP concentrations . Comparable results were produced when the replacement 119 Gln-- > Glu was studied instead of 126 Asn-- > Asp . We infer that the coexistence of the two mutations is responsible for enzyme deficiency in G6PD A- because they act synergistically in causing instability of the enzyme .
