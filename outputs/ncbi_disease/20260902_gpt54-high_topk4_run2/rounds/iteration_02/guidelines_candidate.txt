# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases.  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization.  
- When deciding between **Specific Disease** and **Disease Class**, first ask whether the phrase is being used as an **independently asserted clinical condition**. If it is the condition a patient/organism is said to have, present with, develop, be diagnosed with, or the focal disorder analyzed in the text, annotate it as **Specific Disease**, even if the phrase is headed by a broad common-noun pathology term or another generic clinical noun and is further specified by modifiers.  
- A noun phrase with a **generic pathology head** may still be **Specific Disease** when modifier(s) uniquely subtype or anchor the referent—such as an anatomical site, molecular/genetic marker, pathogen/organism, or other disorder-defining specification—so that the **whole phrase functions as the name of one particular disorder**. This does **not** apply when the same head-modifier structure is used generically to denote a broad category, clinical manifestation, or heterogeneous group of conditions rather than a single disease-level entity.  
- This also applies when the phrase appears as the complement of a predicate that introduces a clinical presentation, manifestation, or etiologic/causal relation, **as long as** the phrase denotes an abnormal pathological, symptomatic, deficiency, or functional-failure state of the patient/organism and functions as a clinical condition in its own right.  
- A disease expression that functions as the **complement/argument of a relational element**—such as a preposition, verb, or participial form—should be labeled according to its disease status under this rule, typically as **Specific Disease**, rather than as **Modifier**. By contrast, direct attributive attachment to a nearby head noun is handled under Rule 3.  
- A noun phrase headed by a **functional-failure expression**—for example, loss, reduction, or absence of **activity** or **function**—and linked by a prepositional complement to the affected gene, enzyme, or protein should be annotated as **Specific Disease** when the whole phrase is used to denote a **stable pathological disorder state**. This narrower rule takes precedence over the broader pathological-state **Disease Class** rule below. Do **not** apply it when the same wording is used only as a mechanistic, assay, experimental, or generic biochemical description.  
- If the same form is used only **taxonomically or descriptively**—for example, as a general category, subtype, or manifestation/feature of another asserted disease—annotate it as **Disease Class** instead **only if it still functions as a disease/condition mention in the text**. If it is merely a feature, consequence, or mechanistic description and does not function as a disease mention in its own right, do **not** annotate it.  
- More descriptive phrases headed by a **state/process nominalization** or other **condition-denoting noun** may also be annotated as **Disease Class** when they explicitly denote an abnormal, deficient, excessive, malformed, or otherwise pathological biological state, especially in definitional, characterization, causal, or resultative constructions, **provided that** the phrase does **not** independently assert a single clinical disease, is not covered by the narrower functional-failure **Specific Disease** rule above, and functions as a pathological-condition mention in the text.  
- This includes **pathological physiological states or deficit phenotypes**, including compositional descriptions built from an abnormality modifier plus an anatomical/state noun, or a deficit/depletion head followed by a prepositional complement, when the phrase is used as the **clinical/pathophysiologic condition** itself. Do **not** apply this rule when the phrase refers only to a normal biological process, a developmental or molecular mechanism, a molecular/anatomical entity, an experimental manipulation, or a local feature/consequence that does not function as a disease mention in its own right.  
- The **Specific Disease** decision takes precedence over the broader **Disease Class** rules above. If a phrase satisfies the independently asserted clinical-condition test or the narrower functional-failure **Specific Disease** condition, annotate it as **Specific Disease**, not **Disease Class**.  
- Do **not** apply these rules to phrases that denote only a normal biological function, a molecular mechanism, a biochemical activity/measurement, a pathological consequence that does not function as a disease mention, or a gene/protein/entity, cause, mechanism, or variant reference without an expressed clinical-state meaning.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**

**Additional Example**  
> Coats disease is characterized by retinal telangiectasis.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Coats disease” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “retinal telangiectasis” as **Specific Disease** when it is used as the independently discussed clinical condition

**Additional Example**  
> Both patients had a family history of ovarian cancer.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “ovarian cancer” as **Specific Disease** because it is the complement of “of,” not a direct noun modifier

---

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.
- This includes disease mentions that modify nouns such as “cases,” “patients,” “families,” “kindreds,” “population,” “study,” “gene,” or “protein.” Even if the disease is central to the text, a mention used only modifierially in these constructions remains **Modifier**.  
- A disease expression is **Modifier** only when it is in **direct attributive attachment** to a head noun (for example, as a prenominal or compound nominal modifier) with no intervening relational layer.  
- If the disease expression instead functions as the complement/argument of a relational element—such as in “history of ovarian cancer,” “patients with SLS,” or “affected by Norrie disease”—it is **not** a **Modifier** merely because another noun is nearby; label it according to Rule 1 instead.  
- In the special case of a compositional disease phrase of the form **[status attribute] + [pathological-state noun] + of + [target]**, a detachable immediately preceding status term that expresses only extent, severity, zygosity, or carrier state is annotated as **Modifier** when it is not part of the lexicalized disease name; annotate the core disease span separately under Rule 5.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

**Additional Example**  
> Germline BRCA1 alterations in a population-based series of ovarian cancer cases.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “ovarian cancer” as **Modifier** because it directly modifies “cases”

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.
- If a disease mention is a compositional noun phrase headed by a pathological-state noun followed by an **of**-prepositional complement naming a molecular, cellular, or anatomical target, annotate the head noun together with its **of**-complement as the core disease span.  
- If an immediately preceding prenominal term only expresses a detachable status attribute such as extent, severity, zygosity, or carrier state, exclude that term from the core **Specific Disease** span and annotate it separately as **Modifier**.  
- The same minimum-span rule applies to headed expressions of **functional failure** such as loss, reduction, or absence of activity/function of a named target **when Rule 1 indicates that the whole phrase is being used as a disease mention**. Include the head expression together with its prepositional complement in the core span.  
- Do **not** split off a preceding term when it is an inseparable part of a conventional lexicalized disease name or synonym.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

**Additional Example**  
> In “complete deficiency of C7,” annotate “deficiency of C7” as **Specific Disease** and “complete” as **Modifier**.

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - When a pathogen/organism term is part of a larger phrase that functions as the name of a disorder, annotate the **full disorder phrase** under Rule 1, but do **not** annotate the organism token alone.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**  
   - Very general terms such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” etc. were excluded **when they stand alone or do not by themselves denote a sufficiently specific pathological condition**.  
   - However, if such a term is sufficiently modified so that the **whole phrase** denotes an abnormal clinical/pathological state, annotate the full phrase as **Specific Disease** or **Disease Class** according to Rule 1.  
   - A generic pathology head may therefore still be annotated when its modifiers uniquely anchor the phrase to one disorder-level entity or otherwise make the whole phrase function as a pathological-condition mention.  
   - However, the terms “cancer” and “tumor” were retained.

5. **Do not annotate references to biological processes**  
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis.”  
   - Also do **not** annotate normal biological functions, molecular mechanisms, biochemical activities/measurements, gene/protein-level consequences, or cause/variant descriptions that do not function as clinical-condition mentions.  
   - **Examples**: “retinal angiogenesis,” “absence of the FMR1 protein,” and “deficient activity of fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase” are **not** annotated.  
   - Likewise, “mutations,” “polymorphisms,” “alterations,” and similar mechanism/variant terms are not annotated unless the disease/condition itself is explicitly named.  
   - A nominalized phrase is annotated only when it denotes a **pathological condition/state** rather than a mechanism, function, or process.  
   - This means that deficit or functional-failure wording is **not automatically** a disease mention. Annotate such a phrase only when Rule 1 indicates that the whole phrase is being used as a stable disorder state of the patient/organism; if the same wording is used only mechanistically, experimentally, or as a biochemical readout, do **not** annotate it.

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

---

## Examples

1. **“Insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus”**  
   - **Specific Disease**  
   - Prefer the whole string.

2. **CDH1 mutations predispose to early onset “colorectal cancer.”**  
   - “early onset” may or may not be part of the disease mention, depending on:  
     1. If there is a UMLS concept specifying this as a separate form of disease  
     2. If the annotator believes it should be included

3. **Human “X-linked recessive disorder”**

4. **Her fresh serum lacked complement-mediated bactericidal activity against Neisseria gonorrhoeae.**

5. **“Huntington disease” (“HD”)**  
   - The long form and the short form constitute two separate mentions.

6. **C7 deficiency**  
   - “This report represents the first cases of C7 deficiency associated with infectious complications.”  
   - “infectious complications” is too general a term; C7 deficiency is a **Specific Disease**.  
   - If the text instead reads “complete deficiency of C7,” annotate “deficiency of C7” as **Specific Disease** and “complete” as **Modifier**.

7. **“colorectal, endometrial, and ovarian cancers”**  
   - Considered one **Composite Mention** of several Specific Diseases.

8. **WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (“Wilms tumor,” “mesothelioma,” “leukemias,” and “breast cancer”) and nonneoplastic (“glomerulosclerosis”) disease.**  
   - Potential disease mentions include: “neoplastic disease,” “nonneoplastic disease,” “Wilms tumor,” “mesothelioma,” “leukemias,” “breast cancer,” and “glomerulosclerosis.”  
   - “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are **not** annotated because these phrases are interrupted by nested mentions.

9. **Tumorigenesis and cancerogenesis**  
   - These refer to processes, not diseases.

10. **“autosomal recessive disease”**  
    - Refers to a family of diseases that can be passed down genetically → **Disease Class** category.

11. **Large phenotypic variability with “convulsive disorders,” “motor retardation,” and “mental retardation.”**  
    - These are “grey area” terms: they may not correspond to a single **Specific Disease**, so they are annotated as **Disease Class** category if appropriate.  
    - If a similar phrase is instead used as an independently asserted clinical condition of the patient/organism, annotate it as **Specific Disease**.

12. **Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome (BFLS) is a syndromal X-linked mental retardation.**  
    - “Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome” = **Specific Disease**  
    - “X-linked mental retardation syndrome” = a family of diseases (i.e., **Disease Class**)

13. **Acute meningococcal pericarditis**  
    - When used as the name of one particular disorder, annotate as **Specific Disease** even though the head noun is a broad pathology term.  
    - This exists as a separate concept in UMLS.

14. **Acute Neisseria infection**  
    - May or may not include “acute” depending on context.

15. **Classical galactosemia**  
    - Includes “classical” because it corresponds to a particular form of the disease.  
    - This contrasts with detachable status terms in phrases like “complete deficiency of C7,” where the status term is not part of the core disease span.

16. **Inherited spinocerebellar ataxia**  
    - May or may not include “inherited,” depending on the annotator.

17. **“Adenomatous polyps of the colon and rectum”**  
    - **Composite Mention**

18. **Fibroepithelial or epithelial hyperplasias**  
    - **Composite Mention**

19. **Stage II or stage III colorectal cancer**  
    - **Composite Mention**

20. **“retinal telangiectasis”**  
    - When used as the independently discussed clinical condition, this is **Specific Disease**, even though it is headed by a broad pathology term.

21. **“unilateral retinal telangiectasis”**  
    - When used as a conventional disease name or synonym, annotate the whole phrase as **Specific Disease**.  
    - Do **not** split off “unilateral” in this case.

22. **“ovarian cancer cases,” “ovarian cancer population,” “ovarian cancer study,” “fragile X syndrome patients,” and “Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein”**  
    - In these constructions, the disease phrase is **Modifier**, not **Specific Disease**, because it directly modifies another noun.

23. **“absence of the FMR1 protein”**  
    - Not annotated, because it is a molecular consequence rather than a clinical-condition mention.

24. **“deficient activity of fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase”**  
    - Not annotated, because it denotes biochemical activity rather than a disease mention.

25. **“family history of ovarian cancer”**  
    - “ovarian cancer” is **Specific Disease** because it is the complement of “of,” not a direct attributive modifier.

26. **“exudative retinal detachment”**  
    - When used as a pathological resultative state or characterization rather than as an independently asserted single disease, annotate as **Disease Class**.

27. **A phrase of the form “loss/reduction/absence of activity or function of X”**  
    - Annotate as **Specific Disease** only when the whole phrase denotes a stable disorder state in the patient/organism.  
    - Do **not** annotate when it is merely mechanistic, experimental, or a biochemical readout.
