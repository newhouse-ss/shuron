# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases, or that are used generically, taxonomically, definitionally, or non-referentially to describe a disease category or abnormal clinical/pathological condition rather than a particular diagnosed condition.  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition, or that in context refer to one instantiated diagnosis or one contextually narrowed subtype that does not include further categorization.  
- A noun phrase headed by a generic pathology term or abnormal-state/symptom noun (e.g., “disease,” “disorder,” “cancer,” “tumor,” “deficiency,” “infection,” “telangiectasis,” “retardation”) is **not automatically** a **Disease Class**. If the **full mention** is used referentially to denote an instantiated diagnosis or a contextually narrowed subtype—often signaled by restrictive modifiers or by patient-case, family-history, manifestation, association, etiologic, or anatomic context—annotate it as **Specific Disease**.  
- Do **not** apply the previous bullet when the same phrase is used generically, taxonomically, definitionally, or in non-referential characterization/etiologic description; in those cases annotate it as **Disease Class**.  
- In particular, in definitional, characterization, or causal/etiologic context, if a noun phrase denotes an abnormal clinical or pathological state of the disorder/patient but is not used referentially as one particular diagnosed condition, annotate it as **Disease Class** even when its head is a generic state/process/symptom noun rather than a canonical disease label.  
- A noun phrase headed by a deficit/absence nominalization or reduced-function expression (e.g., “deficiency,” “absence,” “loss,” “reduced activity,” “deficient activity”) and completed by a specific molecular entity (e.g., an enzyme, gene, protein, gene product, or complement component) is **not automatically excluded** from disease annotation. If the **full phrase** is used referentially to denote a pathological deficiency state or named deficiency disorder—often signaled by disease-definition, causation, characterization, or responsibility-for-condition context—annotate the whole phrase as **Specific Disease**.  
- Do **not** annotate the previous type of phrase when it refers only to an isolated biochemical finding, molecular measurement, mechanistic explanation, or laboratory abnormality rather than to the disorder/pathological condition itself.  
- Outside the deficit/absence/reduced-function case described above, do **not** apply the previous bullets when the phrase refers only to a normal biological process, a mechanistic biological process, a molecular/substance entity or its level/activity, or an isolated descriptive finding that is **not** presented as a pathological condition of the patient/disorder. Such phrases are **not** disease mentions.  
- If the disease string is functioning syntactically as a modifier of another concept, use the **Modifier** category instead of **Specific Disease** or **Disease Class**.  
- When a surrounding noun phrase contains extra descriptive material, decide whether the mention is **Specific Disease** or **Disease Class** based on the disease- or pathological-state-denoting span itself; determine the exact span using Rule 5.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**

**Additional examples**  
> Both patients had a family history of ovarian cancer.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “ovarian cancer” as **Specific Disease** because it refers to an instantiated diagnosed condition in family-history context.

> Coats disease is characterized by abnormal retinal vascular development (so-called retinal telangiectasis).

- **Annotate**:  
  - “retinal telangiectasis” as **Specific Disease** when it is used referentially as the named condition.

> Sjogren-Larsson syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by ichthyosis, mental retardation, and spasticity.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “autosomal recessive disorder” as **Disease Class**  
  - “mental retardation” as **Disease Class**  
  because these phrases are used definitionally/generically rather than as a particular diagnosed condition.

> This report represents the first cases of C7 deficiency associated with infectious complications.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “C7 deficiency” as **Specific Disease**  
  because the phrase denotes a specific deficiency disorder/pathological state rather than merely a laboratory measurement.

> This results in a deficiency of norrin within the developing retina.

- **Do not annotate**  
  because it describes a local mechanistic molecular abnormality rather than naming the disorder/pathological state itself.

> Deficient activity of fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase

- **Do not annotate**  
  because in this context it refers to enzyme activity/biochemical abnormality rather than using the whole phrase as a disease/pathological-state mention.

---

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.  
- This applies even when the underlying disease phrase would otherwise count as a **Specific Disease** or **Disease Class** in context. When the disease words modify another head noun, the annotation category is **Modifier**.  
- Annotate only the disease words themselves, not the larger noun phrase headed by the modified concept.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

**Additional examples**  
- “ovarian cancer” in “ovarian cancer cases” = **Modifier**  
- “ovarian cancer” in “ovarian cancer population” = **Modifier**  
- “ovarian cancer” in “ovarian cancer study” = **Modifier**  
- “ovarian cancer” in “ovarian cancer risk” = **Modifier**  
- “fragile X syndrome” in “fragile X syndrome patients” = **Modifier**  
- “Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome” in “Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein” = **Modifier**

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease or pathological-state mention.  
- Include restrictive modifiers only when they are required to express the specific diagnosed condition or contextually narrowed subtype.  
- When a candidate **Specific Disease** appears inside a larger noun phrase built on a disease-denoting head noun, annotate the smallest contiguous span that can independently identify the disease.  
- Keep a post-head complement (often an “of ...” phrase) only when it is **argumentally necessary**—that is, needed to specify an otherwise generic or incomplete disease/pathology head.  
- For deficit/absence/reduced-function phrases that qualify as disease mentions under Rule 1, retain the complement naming the specific molecular entity when it is needed to identify the pathological deficiency state.  
- Exclude peripheral modifiers or attached prepositional material that merely add descriptive information such as degree, state, laterality, inheritance/carrier status, extent/stage, or anatomical location.  
- Do **include** such modifiers or complements when they are lexically part of the established disease name, correspond to a recognized distinct disease concept or subtype, or are required in context to distinguish the intended disease referent rather than merely describe it.  
- This span-minimization rule does **not** override Rule 3: if the disease words are functioning as modifiers of another head noun, annotate the disease words as **Modifier** rather than extending the span to the full noun phrase.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

**Additional examples**  
> Coats' disease of the retina (unilateral retinal telangiectasis)

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Coats disease” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “retinal telangiectasis” as **Specific Disease**  
  because “of the retina” and “unilateral” add description here but are not needed to independently identify the disease mentions.

> Classical galactosemia

- **Annotate** the full phrase  
  because “classical” is part of a recognized narrower disease form.

> deficiency of C7

- **Retain** the post-head complement when annotating the disease mention  
  because “of C7” is needed to specify the deficiency disorder.

> Adenomatous polyps of the colon and rectum

- **Retain** the post-head complement when annotating the disease mention  
  because “of the colon and rectum” is needed to specify the referents.

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - The same logic applies to other descriptive modifiers when they merely describe the case rather than name a recognized disease form or a required subtype distinction.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**  
   - Very general terms such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” etc. were excluded **when used alone or only as broad pathology heads without enough narrowing context**.  
   - However, a longer phrase headed by one of these terms may still be annotated if the modifiers make it a **Disease Class** or a **Specific Disease** under Rule 1, including in definitional, characterization, or causal/etiologic context when the full phrase denotes an abnormal clinical or pathological state rather than merely a broad outcome label.  
   - Phrases that remain overly broad, nonspecific, or merely descriptive findings/outcomes are not annotated.  
   - The terms “cancer” and “tumor” were retained and are not excluded merely because they are broad pathology heads.

5. **Do not annotate references to biological processes**  
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis.”  
   - This also excludes mechanistic process descriptions such as “retinal angiogenesis,” “retinal vascular development,” “DNA demethylation,” or “gene reactivation” when they are discussed as processes rather than as disease/pathological-state mentions.  
   - This also excludes isolated molecular-function or laboratory-abnormality phrases such as “deficient activity of fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase” when they are used only as biochemical findings or mechanistic descriptions rather than as the pathological condition itself.  
   - However, if a deficit/absence/reduced-function phrase naming a specific molecular entity is used referentially as the pathological deficiency state or disorder, annotate it under Rule 1.

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

---

## Examples

1. **“Insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus”**  
   - **Specific Disease**  
   - Prefer the whole string.

2. **CDH1 mutations predispose to early onset “colorectal cancer.”**  
   - “early onset” may or may not be part of the disease mention, depending on:  
     1. If there is a UMLS concept specifying this as a separate form of disease  
     2. If the annotator believes it should be included

3. **Human “X-linked recessive disorder”**

4. **Her fresh serum lacked complement-mediated bactericidal activity against Neisseria gonorrhoeae.**  
   - “complement-mediated bactericidal activity” is **not** annotated because it refers to a functional activity rather than a disease/pathological-state mention.  
   - The same logic applies to deficit/absence/reduced-function phrases when they are reported only as biochemical findings rather than as the pathological condition itself.

5. **“Huntington disease” (“HD”)**  
   - The long form and the short form constitute two separate mentions.

6. **C7 deficiency**  
   - “This report represents the first cases of C7 deficiency associated with infectious complications.”  
   - “infectious complications” is too general a term and functions as a broad outcome label rather than a disease/pathological-state mention; C7 deficiency is a **Specific Disease** because it denotes a specific deficiency disorder/pathological state.

7. **“colorectal, endometrial, and ovarian cancers”**  
   - Considered one **Composite Mention** of several Specific Diseases.

8. **WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (“Wilms tumor,” “mesothelioma,” “leukemias,” and “breast cancer”) and nonneoplastic (“glomerulosclerosis”) disease.**  
   - Potential disease mentions include: “neoplastic disease,” “nonneoplastic disease,” “Wilms tumor,” “mesothelioma,” “leukemias,” “breast cancer,” and “glomerulosclerosis.”  
   - “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are **not** annotated because these phrases are interrupted by nested mentions.

9. **Tumorigenesis and cancerogenesis**  
   - These refer to processes, not diseases or pathological-state mentions.

10. **“autosomal recessive disease”**  
   - Refers to a family of diseases that can be passed down genetically → **Disease Class** category.

11. **Large phenotypic variability with “convulsive disorders,” “motor retardation,” and “mental retardation.”**  
   - These are “grey area” terms: in definitional, characterization, or causal/etiologic context, they are annotated as **Disease Class** when they denote pathological states rather than molecular measurements, normal/mechanistic processes, or isolated findings.

12. **Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome (BFLS) is a syndromal X-linked mental retardation.**  
   - “Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome” = **Specific Disease**  
   - “X-linked mental retardation syndrome” = a family of diseases (i.e., **Disease Class**)

13. **Acute meningococcal pericarditis**  
   - Exists as a separate concept in UMLS, so the full phrase may be included.

14. **Acute Neisseria infection**  
   - May or may not include “acute” depending on context.

15. **Classical galactosemia**  
   - Includes “classical” because it corresponds to a particular form of the disease.

16. **Inherited spinocerebellar ataxia**  
   - May or may not include “inherited,” depending on the annotator and context.

17. **“Adenomatous polyps of the colon and rectum”**  
   - **Composite Mention**  
   - Retain “of the colon and rectum” because the post-head complement is needed to specify the referents.

18. **Fibroepithelial or epithelial hyperplasias**  
   - **Composite Mention**

19. **Stage II or stage III colorectal cancer**  
   - **Composite Mention**

20. **“ovarian cancer” in family-history versus modifier use**  
   - In “family history of ovarian cancer,” “ovarian cancer” = **Specific Disease** because it denotes a diagnosed condition referentially.  
   - In “ovarian cancer cases,” “ovarian cancer population,” “ovarian cancer study,” or “ovarian cancer risk,” “ovarian cancer” = **Modifier** because it modifies another head noun.

21. **“retinal telangiectasis” and “Coats telangiectasis”**  
   - These can be **Specific Disease** mentions when used referentially as diagnosed conditions, even though “telangiectasis” is a generic pathology head and even though similar abnormal-state phrases may otherwise be **Disease Class** in non-referential use.  
   - In “unilateral retinal telangiectasis,” annotate “retinal telangiectasis” and exclude “unilateral” when it only adds descriptive laterality/state information.  
   - In “Coats disease of the retina,” annotate “Coats disease” and exclude “of the retina” when it only provides additional description rather than distinguishing the disease.

22. **Disease names used to modify other biomedical entities**  
   - In “fragile X syndrome patients,” “fragile X syndrome” = **Modifier**.  
   - In “Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein,” “Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome” = **Modifier**.

23. **Definitional/generic use of broad pathology phrases and abnormal-state phrases**  
   - In “Sjogren-Larsson syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by ichthyosis, mental retardation, and spasticity,”  
      - “autosomal recessive disorder” = **Disease Class**  
      - “mental retardation” = **Disease Class**  
   - These are not treated as **Specific Disease** mentions because they are used definitionally rather than to denote a particular diagnosed condition.  
   - In “deficient activity of fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase,” the phrase is **not** annotated because here it refers to enzyme activity/biochemical abnormality rather than to a disease/pathological-state mention.  
   - By contrast, deficit/absence/reduced-function phrases naming a specific molecular entity can be **Specific Disease** when the full phrase is used referentially as the pathological deficiency state or disorder itself.

24. **Disease mention inside a larger descriptive phrase**  
   - In “the classical form of Coats disease,” annotate “Coats disease” as **Specific Disease** unless the longer expression clearly names a recognized distinct disease concept or a required subtype distinction.
