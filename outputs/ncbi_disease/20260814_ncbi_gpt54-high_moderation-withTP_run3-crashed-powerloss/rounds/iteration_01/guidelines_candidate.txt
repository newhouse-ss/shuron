# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases, or that denote a broad condition category in a generic, taxonomic, phenotypic, or list/definition context.  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization.

- A mention headed by a generic pathology noun (e.g., “disease,” “syndrome,” “disorder,” “deficiency,” “infection,” “cancer,” “tumor,” “pericarditis,” “telangiectasis”) should still be treated as **Specific Disease** when the text instantiates it as a particular disorder. This may happen in either of the following ways:  
  1. The head noun is narrowed by a specifying dependent/modifier, such as an etiologic, anatomical, temporal, severity, inheritance, stage, laterality, eponymic, organism-based, or other restricting modifier.  
  2. The surrounding clause places the mention in a patient-level diagnostic/event frame, such as a condition a patient or case is described as having, presenting with, being affected by, being diagnosed with, or being reported with.
- Do **not** apply the above rule when the same head noun is used only generically or taxonomically, or when it appears in a phenotype list or definition without linguistic cues linking it to a concrete disease instance. In such cases, annotate as **Disease Class** if appropriate.
- This rule determines whether a disease mention is **Specific Disease** or **Disease Class**. If the mention instead functions as a modifier of another concept, use the **Modifier** category (see Rule 3). If one contiguous string refers to multiple diseases, use **Composite Mention** (see Rule 2).
- The **minimum necessary span** rule still applies: include only the tokens needed to express the most specific disease mention.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**

**Additional Examples**  
> A female with a unilateral variant of Coats disease gave birth to a son affected by Norrie disease.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Norrie disease” as **Specific Disease** because the generic head “disease” is instantiated as a particular disorder and appears in a patient-level diagnostic frame (“affected by”).

> Sjogren-Larsson syndrome is characterized by ichthyosis and spasticity.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “ichthyosis” as **Disease Class**  
  - “spasticity” as **Disease Class**  
  because they are used in a phenotypic/list context rather than as concrete disease instances.

---

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

- Use **Composite Mention** whenever one contiguous string refers to multiple separate diseases, even if the phrase appears in a modifier-like environment.

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

**Additional Example**  
> Both patients had a family history of breast or ovarian cancer.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “breast or ovarian cancer” as **Composite Mention**

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.

- For single-disease mentions, this rule applies even if the same string would otherwise qualify as a **Specific Disease** under Rule 1. When a disease mention modifies another concept such as “families,” “kindreds,” “cases,” “patients,” “protein,” “gene,” “risk,” or “population,” label it as **Modifier** rather than **Specific Disease** or **Disease Class**.
- If the contiguous string refers to multiple diseases, use **Composite Mention** instead of **Modifier**.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

**Additional Examples**  
> Germline BRCA1 alterations in a population-based series of ovarian cancer cases.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “ovarian cancer” as **Modifier**

> The molecular basis of Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome: mutation analysis of the fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase gene.

> Autoinhibition and activation mechanisms of the Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome” as **Modifier** when it modifies “protein”

> Most fragile X syndrome patients have expansion of a ( CGG ) ( n ) sequence with > 200 repeats.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “fragile X syndrome” as **Modifier**

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.

- This also applies when a generic pathology head noun is made specific by a narrowing dependent: include only the modifiers needed to identify the concrete disorder.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**  
   - Standalone or weakly specified general pathology nouns such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “disorder,” “deficiency,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” etc. were excluded.  
   - These terms may be annotated only when the phrase as a whole is sufficiently instantiated to denote a particular disorder (see Rule 1) or when it forms an appropriate **Disease Class** mention.  
   - Do **not** annotate them when they remain generic, taxonomic, phenotypic, or definition/list-like, or when they are still too vague to identify a disease-level concept (e.g., “infectious complications”).  
   - However, the terms “cancer” and “tumor” were retained when they participate in a disease mention.

5. **Do not annotate references to **biological processes**  
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis.”

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

---

## Examples

1. **“Insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus”**  
   - **Specific Disease**  
   - Prefer the whole string.

2. **CDH1 mutations predispose to early onset “colorectal cancer.”**  
   - “colorectal cancer” is a **Specific Disease**: the generic head “cancer” is narrowed by the anatomical modifier “colorectal.”  
   - “early onset” may or may not be part of the disease mention, depending on:  
     1. If there is a UMLS concept specifying this as a separate form of disease  
     2. If the annotator believes it should be included

3. **Human “X-linked recessive disorder”**  
   - Exclude “Human” from the span.  
   - “X-linked recessive disorder” is generally **Disease Class** unless the context clearly instantiates one particular disorder.

4. **Her fresh serum lacked complement-mediated bactericidal activity against Neisseria gonorrhoeae.**  
   - Do not annotate the organism name alone unless the disease it causes is clearly being referred to.

5. **“Huntington disease” (“HD”)**  
   - The long form and the short form constitute two separate mentions.

6. **C7 deficiency**  
   - “This report represents the first cases of C7 deficiency associated with infectious complications.”  
   - “C7 deficiency” is a **Specific Disease**: the generic head “deficiency” is sufficiently specified by “C7.”  
   - “infectious complications” is too general a term and is not annotated.

7. **“colorectal, endometrial, and ovarian cancers”**  
   - Considered one **Composite Mention** of several Specific Diseases.

8. **WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (“Wilms tumor,” “mesothelioma,” “leukemias,” and “breast cancer”) and nonneoplastic (“glomerulosclerosis”) disease.**  
   - Potential disease mentions include: “neoplastic disease,” “nonneoplastic disease,” “Wilms tumor,” “mesothelioma,” “leukemias,” “breast cancer,” and “glomerulosclerosis.”  
   - “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are **not** annotated because these phrases are interrupted by nested mentions.

9. **Tumorigenesis and cancerogenesis**  
   - These refer to processes, not diseases.

10. **“autosomal recessive disease”**  
    - Refers to a family of diseases that can be passed down genetically → **Disease Class** category.  
    - This is a taxonomic/generic use, not a concrete disease instance.

11. **Large phenotypic variability with “convulsive disorders,” “motor retardation,” and “mental retardation.”**  
    - These are “grey area” terms: they may not correspond to a single **Specific Disease**, so they are annotated as **Disease Class** category if appropriate.  
    - This remains true especially in phenotypic/list contexts.

12. **Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome (BFLS) is a syndromal X-linked mental retardation.**  
    - “Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome” = **Specific Disease**  
    - “X-linked mental retardation syndrome” = a family of diseases (i.e., **Disease Class**)

13. **Acute meningococcal pericarditis**  
    - **Specific Disease**  
    - A generic head noun can be annotated as **Specific Disease** when it is sufficiently narrowed; this phrase exists as a separate concept in UMLS.

14. **Acute Neisseria infection**  
    - “infection” can be a **Specific Disease** when the phrase is instantiated as a concrete disorder.  
    - “acute” may or may not be included depending on context.

15. **Classical galactosemia**  
    - Includes “classical” because it corresponds to a particular form of the disease.

16. **Inherited spinocerebellar ataxia**  
    - May or may not include “inherited,” depending on whether it is needed to express the specific disorder in context.

17. **“Adenomatous polyps of the colon and rectum”**  
    - **Composite Mention**

18. **Fibroepithelial or epithelial hyperplasias**  
    - **Composite Mention**

19. **Stage II or stage III colorectal cancer**  
    - **Composite Mention**

20. **“ovarian cancer cases,” “fragile X syndrome patients,” and “Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein”**  
    - “ovarian cancer,” “fragile X syndrome,” and “Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome” are **Modifier** mentions because they modify “cases,” “patients,” and “protein,” respectively.

21. **“Norrie disease” and “Coats telangiectasis”**  
    - Both are **Specific Disease**.  
    - Generic heads such as “disease” and “telangiectasis” become **Specific Disease** when instantiated as particular disorders by specifying dependents and/or patient-level diagnostic frames.

22. **“ichthyosis” and “spasticity” in syndrome characterization**  
    - In a sentence such as “Sjogren-Larsson syndrome is characterized by ichthyosis and spasticity,” these are **Disease Class** because they are presented as phenotypic features, not concrete disease instances.
