# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases.  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**  
- **Do not annotate**: “short stature,” “very short limbs,” and “joint problems” as features of the syndrome (see §1b).

#### 1a. Patient-anchored bare disease nouns → Specific Disease
If a disease mention is a bare, unmodified head noun with no proper name, eponym, or acronym (singular or plural), and the noun is predicated of a particular individual, case, or genotype, annotate it as **Specific Disease**, regardless of whether its lexical meaning suggests a superordinate category. Anchoring cues include:
- a presenting or diagnostic predicate (e.g., “presenting with/as X,” “was found to have X”), or
- an etiologic association with a named organism, gene, or agent (e.g., “associated with [gene] mutations,” “X can be associated with [agent]”).

Decide by the referential relation (which individual, case, or genotype the noun is predicated of), not by the head noun’s suffix or hypernym-like meaning.

These anchoring cues also separate this rule from §1e: a bare noun carrying an anchoring cue is never labelled Disease Class by default.

This rule does **not** apply when:
- the noun is a superordinate category grouping multiple named conditions (e.g., “types of X,” “a class of X disorders”). It remains **Disease Class**.
- the noun is a generic trait in a syndrome’s feature description and is not attributed to a particular patient or genotype (e.g., “characterized by X, Y, and Z”). Such traits are governed only by §1b and are otherwise left unlabelled.
- the mention already carries a proper name, eponym, or acronym. The standard Specific Disease rule applies.
- the term functions as a Modifier qualifying another condition name, or as a bare premodifier of a molecular-entity head (e.g., “cancer gene”). These are governed by §3.
- the noun is a general term excluded under “What NOT to Annotate” #4 (e.g., “disease,” “syndrome,” “complications,” “abnormalities”).
- the noun is a bare cohort, family-history, or risk descriptor (e.g., “Caucasian cancer cases,” “a family history of cancer”). See “What NOT to Annotate” #8.
- the span contains a biomolecule complement or a deficit head. These are governed by §1c.

#### 1b. Condition-level manifestations in characterizing predicates → Disease Class
A span is annotated as **Disease Class** when all of the following hold:
- its head noun denotes a condition-level abnormal state or pathological category (e.g., “mental retardation,” “convulsive disorders,” “motor retardation”), rather than a physical sign or a process;
- it is the complement of a disease-characterizing predicate whose subject is the disease mention (or its disease-category head), such as “X is an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by …”, or it is an item in a coordinated list that follows such a predicate;
- it has no eponym, acronym, or gene designation.

This applies whether or not the span carries disease-suffix morphology. It does **not** apply when:
- (a) the span attributively modifies a following disease head noun. It is then a **Modifier** (§3).
- (b) the span is a named or gene-specified disorder. It is then a **Specific Disease**.
- (c) the span is a constituent of a larger composite mention. The composite is annotated under the Composite Mention rules (§2) and is not split.
- (d) the head noun denotes a gene, protein, anatomical structure, measurement, assay, activity, or non-pathological physiological state. A “deficiency of [molecule]” is tagged under this rule only when it describes a generic pathological state, and never when §1c applies.
- (e) the span lies outside a characterizing predicate: in a procedural, measurement, or treatment context; in an appositive or parenthetical gloss; or inside a subordinate mechanistic clause (e.g., “…, which results in …”).
- (f) the span is a physical sign, symptom, or anatomical/developmental process in a feature list (e.g., “spasticity,” “short stature,” “joint problems,” “ichthyosis” as a skin feature, “abnormal retinal vascular development”). These remain unlabelled, including when they would otherwise be picked up by §1e.

Feature-list items that fail any of the criteria above are left unlabelled, even when they are symptom-like.

#### 1c. Nominal deficit phrases name the disease, including a prenominal biomolecule complement → Specific Disease
IF a noun phrase’s head is a nominal deficit or loss-of-function term (such as “deficiency,” “absence,” or “deficient activity” used as a noun) and takes an “of” or “in” complement that names a gene, enzyme, or protein as the defective entity, AND the phrase denotes a congenital or inherited deficiency state, THEN annotate the Specific Disease span as follows:
- **Prenominal biomolecule premodifier:** A premodifying biomolecule name before a final deficit head (e.g., “C7 deficiency,” “glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency”) is annotated as one span, including the premodifier.
- **Postnominal complement:** When the complement follows the head (e.g., “deficiency of factor VIII”), the Postnominal Complement Exclusion below applies.

A congenital or inherited reading is signalled by genetic context, such as “inherited,” “congenital,” “germline,” “X-linked,” “autosomal,” or an established inherited enzyme deficiency (e.g., G6PD deficiency).

**Postnominal Complement Exclusion.** A postnominal “of/in” phrase whose object names a substance, gene, protein, enzyme, or body site complements the head rather than forming part of a compound name. The Specific Disease span ends at the head noun and includes only its prenominal premodifiers; the complement stays outside the span. Because a bare “deficiency” is a general term (What NOT #4), the span is annotated only when an inherited-reading premodifier is present. For example, “congenital deficiency of factor VIII” yields the span “congenital deficiency,” and “factor VIII” is left outside. A deficit head with no inherited-reading premodifier (e.g., “enzyme deficiency in G6PD A-”) is not annotated.

The Postnominal Complement Exclusion does **not** apply when:
- the object noun is prenominal and forms a compound with the head, and the whole compound is the conventional name (e.g., “G6PD deficiency,” “factor VIII deficiency”);
- the deficit term is adjectival or participial (e.g., “G6PD deficient,” “X-deficient”). Annotate these as **Modifier**;
- the complement is multi-entity or coordinated. These fall under the Composite Mention rules (§2);
- the title is fixed or lexicalized and the “of/in” phrase cannot be detached without changing the name. Check such titles against the Examples before applying this rule.

This rule does **not** apply when:
- (a) the deficit term is adjectival or predicative (e.g., “G6PD deficient,” “[enzyme] deficient”). Annotate these as **Modifier**.
- (b) the head is a bare measurement, assay, level, or concentration term that lacks a deficit sense. A deficit qualifier on such a head, as in “deficient activity,” does count as a deficit head.
- (c) the construction is a generic biochemical or mechanistic description with no congenital or inherited reading, including acquired or somatic lesions (e.g., “a somatic mutation … results in a deficiency of [protein]”). These are also excluded from §1e (What NOT #10).
- (d) the item is an isolated symptom in a coordinated presentation list (e.g., “ichthyosis, mental retardation, spasticity, and deficient activity of [enzyme]”). Such items are outside this pattern and must not be used to widen it. They are governed by §1b where that applies, and are otherwise left unlabelled.

#### 1d. Coreferential disorder-name rule: finding-type heads → Specific Disease
IF a noun phrase has a finding-type head (a sign, lesion, symptom, process, or measured-activity noun, optionally with adjectival, prepositional, or genitive modifiers) AND one of the following holds, THEN annotate the full phrase, including its modifiers, as **Specific Disease**:
- (i) **Eponymic or acronymic anchor:** the phrase is combined with an eponymic or acronymic element (e.g., “Coats telangiectasis”).
- (ii) **Naming restatement:** the phrase is restated as a disorder name elsewhere in the same document through a parenthetical, appositive, or “also termed”-type marker, and that restatement names the disorder itself. A “so-called” or parenthetical phrase that attaches a manifestation to a disorder is a descriptive gloss, not a naming restatement (What NOT #9).
- (iii) **Canonical equivalent:** the phrase is a “[finding-noun] of [enzyme or protein]” construction that is semantically equivalent to a canonical “[enzyme or protein] deficiency” disorder name stated in the same document.

**Negative constraints:**
- A bare sign or symptom in a coordinated presenting-feature list, with no coreferent disorder name or eponymic anchor in the document, is not annotated (§1b(f); What NOT #7). If the surrounding context is truncated, flag the item for review as a probable annotation error rather than labelling it.
- Do not apply this rule to a standalone anatomical or pathological description without a coreference or synonymy signal.
- Do not apply this rule when the phrase denotes a category or class of disorders. Such phrases are **Disease Class** (§1b).

#### 1e. Unanchored condition terms default to Disease Class
IF a condition-denoting span (a bare disease head noun, a descriptive or pathological noun phrase, or an “X of Y” state noun phrase whose head denotes a condition) occurs in a non-prenominal position, such as a complement or adjunct that describes a manifestation, consequence, association, or characterization of a condition, AND it has no anchor and no site-plus-disease compound, and no disease, syndrome, or dysplasia head identifying a particular disorder, THEN annotate it as **Disease Class**, regardless of whether the surrounding clause is descriptive rather than nominal.

A span is **anchored** if it has (i) a proper name, eponym, or acronym; (ii) a named-entity head; or (iii) the patient-, case-, or genotype-anchoring cues of §1a. Anchored spans are handled by §1a or the standard Specific Disease rule, never by this rule.

This rule does **not** apply when:
- (a) the phrase is anchored (see above). It is **Specific Disease** under §1a or the standard rule.
- (b) the phrase is prenominal and modifies a following noun, such as a noun-noun compound or attributive premodifier. It is **Modifier** (§3).
- (c) the span is part of a coordinated or multi-condition construction that refers to several conditions at once. It is **Composite Mention** (§2). This clause does not extend to family-history or cohort descriptors (What NOT #8), which are not annotated as composites.
- (d) the phrase denotes a non-condition entity, such as an anatomical structure, gene, protein, drug, organism, measurement, assay, activity, or biological process (§1b(d); What NOT #1, #5).
- (e) the span is a physical sign, symptom, or anatomical/developmental process in a feature list (§1b(f)).
- (f) the span lies in a subordinate mechanistic clause (e.g., “…, which results in …”), or is a parenthetical or appositive gloss describing a feature or manifestation of a disease (What NOT #9), or sits in a procedural, measurement, or treatment context (§1b(e)). The “consequence” cue applies only to a condition-level manifestation of the disease subject under a characterizing predicate, not to a mechanistic clause.
- (g) the span is a general term (What NOT #4), or sits inside a bare cohort, family-history, or risk descriptor (What NOT #8).
- (h) the span is a deficit of a named biomolecule handled by §1c, including the Postnominal Complement Exclusion, or a deficit described as an acquired, somatic, or mechanistic consequence (§1c(c)).
- (i) the span is a finding-type phrase handled by §1d.

**Validity check:** Prenominal position, anchoring, and coordination decide between Modifier, Specific Disease, and Composite Mention. This rule supplies only the Disease Class label for the remaining unanchored condition terms. It does not alter any entity definition.

#### Precedence between rules
Where more than one rule could label the same span, apply them in the following order. Each later rule excludes spans already handled by an earlier one.
1. **Narrowest (explicit molecular, positional, or coreference cue):** the molecular-head premodifier rule (§3), the nested cohort/nomenclature Modifier rule (§3), the nominal deficit rule with its Postnominal Complement Exclusion (§1c), and the coreferential disorder-name rule (§1d).
2. **Narrow:** the condition-level manifestation rule (§1b). It requires a characterizing predicate and a non-sign head.
3. **Broad:** the patient-anchored bare-noun rule (§1a). It excludes spans handled by rules 1 and 2.
4. **Residual default:** the unanchored condition-term rule (§1e). It applies only to spans not handled by rules 1–3, and it excludes anchored, prenominal, composite, and non-condition spans.

Between the new principles: §1d is narrower than §1e because it requires an explicit eponymic, restatement, or canonical-name signal. §1e therefore excludes every span that §1d handles. The Postnominal Complement Exclusion is narrower than the complement-inclusive wording it replaces, so §1c is ranked first and §1e excludes its spans. Where the nested-modifier rule (§3) and §1e would both label a prenominal disease span as Modifier, they agree, and §1e defers to §3 so that each span is labelled once. Where the nested-modifier rule’s nomenclature wording conflicts with the official gene-name exclusion (What NOT #11), the exclusion governs.

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

Coordinated condition terms that refer to several conditions at once (§1e(c)) are annotated as Composite Mention. Coordinated family-history or cohort descriptors are not composites (What NOT #8).

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

#### Disease span as leftmost premodifier of a molecular-entity head → Modifier
IF a disease-denoting span (a full disease name or its acronym) occupies the leftmost premodifier slot of a noun phrase whose head denotes a molecular entity (a gene, protein, or similar nomenclature-bearing noun, including forms with numeric or designator suffixes), THEN annotate the disease span as **Modifier**. The molecular status of the head does not license an O label for the disease span.

- “ancient SLS genes” → “SLS” as **Modifier**
- “the DTD gene” → “DTD” as **Modifier**

This rule does **not** apply when:
- the disease-denoting span is the phrase head itself, or appears inside a postmodifying prepositional phrase (e.g., a “gene for [disease]” relation);
- the candidate is a gene or protein symbol that carries no disease reading in context (e.g., BRCA1, CSF1R);
- the leftmost premodifier is a non-disease term (e.g., anatomical, chemical, or organism terms);
- a general term such as “disease” occupies the premodifier slot (e.g., “disease genes”), because it is not a disease-denoting span;
- the disease-like words are part of an official gene name, meaning the expansion of a gene symbol. In “breast cancer susceptibility gene 1 (BRCA1),” the words “breast cancer” name the gene and are not annotated. Descriptive references to “the [disease] gene” are annotated as above.

**Scope:** This rule governs only the label of the disease span. It does not authorize labelling the molecular-entity head or the full identifier as **Specific Disease** or **Disease Class**, and it does not alter **Composite Mention** boundaries. It is consistent with the **Modifier** definition, in which a disease term functions adjectivally within a larger noun phrase.

#### Nested disease span in attributive position of a cohort or nomenclature phrase → Modifier
IF a disease-denoting span (a named disease, a disease abbreviation, or a specific disease-category term such as “colorectal cancer”) appears in pre-head, attributive position inside a larger noun phrase whose head is a gene or protein name, or a cohort or population noun (e.g., family, kindreds, cases, patients, probands, population, series, cohort), THEN annotate that span as **Modifier**. The span is labelled even when it is embedded in a nomenclature string, except for the official expansion of a gene symbol (What NOT #11), which is not annotated.

The label of the nested span does not depend on the head type (gene/protein versus cohort), on any particular disease name or abbreviation, or on the nomenclature wrapper. The enclosing head is not itself labelled, and the span is not merged into the gene, protein, or cohort label.

- “colorectal cancer kindreds” → “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**
- “ovarian cancer cases” → “ovarian cancer” as **Modifier**

This rule does **not** apply when:
- the disease-denoting span is the phrase head, or is the complement of a preposition or verb that names the condition itself (e.g., “kindreds with SLS”). Such spans follow the Specific Disease and Disease Class definitions;
- the head of the whole phrase is itself a disease or condition noun, so the entire string is a single disease mention;
- the pre-head material is not disease-denoting: gene symbols with no disease reading, numerals, anatomical terms, or technical descriptors (e.g., “CSF1R gene”);
- the premodifier is a general term such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” or “complications” (What NOT #4), or a bare, unspecified “cancer” or “tumor” labelling a cohort or family history (What NOT #8; e.g., “91 Caucasian cancer cases”);
- the disease-like words are the official expansion of a gene symbol (What NOT #11; e.g., “breast cancer susceptibility gene 1 (BRCA1)”).

**Scope:** This rule governs only the label of the nested span. It does not label the gene, protein, or cohort head, or the full identifier, and it does not alter Composite Mention boundaries.

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**  
   - Very general terms such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” etc. were excluded.  
   - A bare “deficiency” is general, including “enzyme deficiency” and “deficiency in [gene]” without an inherited-reading premodifier. A “deficiency” qualifies only under §1c, either with a named biomolecule premodifier or with an inherited-reading premodifier whose postnominal complement is detached.  
   - However, the terms “cancer” and “tumor” were retained (but see #8 for bare cohort descriptors).  
   - These exclusions also hold under §1a, §1b, §1d, §1e, and the premodifier slot rules in §3 (e.g., “disease genes,” “disease locus”).

5. **Do not annotate references to biological processes**  
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis.”  
   - Developmental, vascular, or other anatomical process descriptions (e.g., “abnormal retinal vascular development”) are not annotated, even inside a disease-characterizing predicate (§1b(f)).

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

7. **Do not annotate physical signs and symptoms in feature lists**  
   - Signs and symptoms listed as features of a syndrome (e.g., “spasticity,” “short stature,” “joint problems”) are not annotated. Only condition-level items may be **Disease Class** (§1b).  
   - A finding-type phrase in such a list is annotated only when §1d applies: an eponymic anchor, a naming restatement, or a canonical equivalent stated in the same document.

8. **Do not annotate bare cohort, family-history, or risk descriptors**  
   - A bare, unspecified “cancer” used to label a study population or a family history (e.g., “91 Caucasian cancer cases,” “a family history of cancer”) is not annotated. Such phrases are not Composite Mentions and are excluded from §1e and from the nested-modifier rule in §3.  
   - When the disease is named in such a phrase (e.g., “ovarian cancer cases,” “a family history of ovarian cancer”), the named disease is a **Modifier** (§3).

9. **Do not annotate descriptive glosses**  
   - A parenthetical or appositive phrase that describes a feature or manifestation of a disease, rather than naming a disease, is not annotated (e.g., “(so-called retinal telangiectasis),” “(unilateral retinal telangiectasis)” in a title, “(exudative retinal detachment)”).  
   - A “so-called” phrase that attaches a manifestation to a disorder is a gloss, not a naming restatement under §1d(ii).  
   - This does not apply to abbreviation or synonym parentheses, which are annotated under §6 (e.g., “Huntington disease” (“HD”)).

10. **Do not annotate acquired, somatic, or mechanistic deficiency descriptions**  
    - A deficiency of a protein described as the mechanistic consequence of a somatic or acquired lesion (e.g., “a somatic mutation … results in a deficiency of [protein]”) is not annotated (§1c(c)).  
    - This also holds inside a subordinate “which results in” or “resulting in” clause (§1b(e), §1e(f)).

11. **Do not annotate disease words inside official gene names**  
    - The disease-like words in an official gene name (the expansion of a gene symbol) are not annotated (§3). This exclusion takes precedence over the nested-modifier rule in §3.

## Examples

1. **“Insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus”**  
   - **Specific Disease**  
   - Prefer the whole string.

2. **CDH1 mutations predispose to early onset “colorectal cancer.”**  
   - “early onset” may or may not be part of the disease mention, depending on:  
     1. If there is a UMLS concept specifying this as a separate form of disease  
     2. If the annotator believes it should be included

3. **Human “X-linked recessive disorder”**

4. **Her fresh serum lacked complement-mediated bactericidal activity against Neisseria gonorrhoeae.**

5. **“Huntington disease” (“HD”)**  
   - The long form and the short form constitute two separate mentions.

6. **C7 deficiency**  
   - “This report represents the first cases of C7 deficiency associated with infectious complications.”  
   - “infectious complications” is too general a term; C7 deficiency is a **Specific Disease**.  
   - “C7 deficiency” is a biomolecule premodifier with a deficit head, so it falls under §1c.

7. **“colorectal, endometrial, and ovarian cancers”**  
   - Considered one **Composite Mention** of several Specific Diseases.

8. **WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (“Wilms tumor,” “mesothelioma,” “leukemias,” and “breast cancer”) and nonneoplastic (“glomerulosclerosis”) disease.**  
   - Potential disease mentions include: “neoplastic disease,” “nonneoplastic disease,” “Wilms tumor,” “mesothelioma,” “leukemias,” “breast cancer,” and “glomerulosclerosis.”  
   - “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are **not** annotated because these phrases are interrupted by nested mentions.

9. **Tumorigenesis and cancerogenesis**  
   - These refer to processes, not diseases.

10. **“autosomal recessive disease”**  
    - Refers to a family of diseases that can be passed down genetically → **Disease Class** category.

11. **Large phenotypic variability with “convulsive disorders,” “motor retardation,” and “mental retardation.”**  
    - These are “grey area” terms: they may not correspond to a single **Specific Disease**, so they are annotated as **Disease Class** category if appropriate (see §1b for the characterizing-predicate test).

12. **Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome (BFLS) is a syndromal X-linked mental retardation.**  
    - “Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome” = **Specific Disease**  
    - “X-linked mental retardation syndrome” = a family of diseases (i.e., **Disease Class**)

13. **Acute meningococcal pericarditis**  
    - Exists as a separate concept in UMLS.

14. **Acute Neisseria infection**  
    - May or may not include “acute” depending on context.

15. **Classical galactosemia**  
    - Includes “classical” because it corresponds to a particular form of the disease.

16. **Inherited spinocerebellar ataxia**  
    - May or may not include “inherited,” depending on the annotator.

17. **“Adenomatous polyps of the colon and rectum”**  
    - **Composite Mention**

18. **Fibroepithelial or epithelial hyperplasias**  
    - **Composite Mention**

19. **Stage II or stage III colorectal cancer**  
    - **Composite Mention**

20. **“Two children presented with leukemia.”**  
    - “leukemia” = **Specific Disease** (bare head noun with a presenting predicate; §1a). The anchoring cue places it outside §1e.  
    - **“types of leukemia”**: “leukemia” = **Disease Class** (superordinate category; §1a).

21. **“Neuropathy was associated with [gene] mutations in these probands.”**  
    - “Neuropathy” = **Specific Disease** (etiologic association with a named gene; §1a).

22. **“Patients with deficiency or complications were excluded.”**  
    - Not annotated. “deficiency” and “complications” are general terms (What NOT #4). Neither the patient-anchored rule nor §1e overrides them.

23. **“91 Caucasian cancer cases”; “a family history of cancer”; “ovarian cancer cases”**  
    - Bare “cancer” in the first two is not annotated (What NOT #8).  
    - “ovarian cancer” in the third is a **Modifier** (§3).

24. **“Congenital factor VIII deficiency”; “congenital deficiency of factor VIII”**  
    - “Congenital factor VIII deficiency” = **Specific Disease** for the whole string (biomolecule premodifier with a deficit head, inherited reading; §1c).  
    - “congenital deficiency of factor VIII” = **Specific Disease** for “congenital deficiency” only. “factor VIII” is a postnominal complement outside the span (§1c, Postnominal Complement Exclusion).  
    - A bare “deficiency of factor VIII” with no inherited-reading premodifier is not annotated (What NOT #4).

25. **“the G6PD deficient phenotype”** and **“G6PD deficiency”**  
    - “G6PD deficient” = **Modifier** (adjectival deficit term; §1c(a)).  
    - “G6PD deficiency” = **Specific Disease** (§1c; prenominal compound, so the Postnominal Complement Exclusion does not apply).

26. **“A somatic mutation in [gene] results in a deficiency of [protein] within the developing retina.”**  
    - Not annotated. The deficiency is an acquired, mechanistic consequence of a somatic lesion, not a congenital or inherited state (§1c(c); What NOT #10). §1e does not apply because the span sits in a mechanistic clause and is a deficit of a named biomolecule (§1e(f), (h)).

27. **“the ancient SLS genes”; “the DTD gene”**  
    - “SLS” and “DTD” = **Modifier** (leftmost premodifier of a molecular-entity head; §3).

28. **“breast cancer susceptibility gene 1 (BRCA1)”**  
    - “breast cancer” is not annotated. The words are part of the official gene name, the expansion of the BRCA1 symbol (§3; What NOT #11). BRCA1 itself is not a disease mention.

29. **“Sjogren-Larsson syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by ichthyosis, mental retardation, spasticity, and deficient activity of fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase.”**  
    - “Sjogren-Larsson syndrome” = **Specific Disease**.  
    - “autosomal recessive disorder” = **Disease Class**.  
    - “mental retardation” = **Disease Class** (condition-level item in a list following a characterizing predicate; §1b).  
    - “ichthyosis” and “spasticity” are physical signs and are not annotated (§1b(f)).  
    - “deficient activity of fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase” is an isolated symptom in a presentation list and is not annotated (§1c(d)). §1d is not triggered: the phrase has no eponymic anchor and no naming restatement, and the document contains no canonical “[enzyme] deficiency” disorder name.

30. **“Coats disease is characterized by abnormal retinal vascular development (so-called retinal telangiectasis) which results in massive intraretinal and subretinal lipid accumulation (exudative retinal detachment).”**  
    - “Coats disease” = **Specific Disease** (eponym).  
    - In that sentence, nothing else is annotated. The predicate complement is a vascular/developmental process (§1b(f)). The “which results in” clause is a subordinate mechanistic clause (§1b(e), §1e(f)). The parentheticals are descriptive glosses (What NOT #9), and “so-called retinal telangiectasis” is not a naming restatement under §1d(ii).  
    - Likewise, in “Coats’ disease of the retina (unilateral retinal telangiectasis),” only “Coats’ disease” is annotated.  
    - Elsewhere in the same abstract, “Coats telangiectasis” is **Specific Disease** (§1d(i)). “a deficiency of norrin” in the “results in” clause is not annotated (§1c(c); §1e(f), (h)).

31. **“We suggest that Coats telangiectasis is secondary to somatic mutation in the NDP gene.”**  
    - “Coats telangiectasis” = **Specific Disease**. The eponymic anchor makes the finding-type head a disorder name (§1d(i)).

32. **“Enzyme deficiency in G6PD A- was responsible for instability.”**  
    - Not annotated. “enzyme deficiency” is a bare, general deficit term with no inherited-reading premodifier (What NOT #4; §1c). The postnominal “in G6PD A-” phrase does not make it a Specific Disease.

33. **“colorectal cancer kindreds”; “kindreds with SLS”**  
    - “colorectal cancer” in “colorectal cancer kindreds” = **Modifier** (cohort-noun head; nested rule in §3).  
    - “SLS” in “kindreds with SLS” = **Specific Disease**. It is the complement of a preposition that names the condition itself, so the nested rule does not apply.
