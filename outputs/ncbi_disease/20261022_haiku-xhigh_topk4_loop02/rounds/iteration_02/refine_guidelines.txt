# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases.  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**

#### Anchored mentions: Specific Disease despite generic wording
A mention is labeled **Specific Disease** even when its head noun is generic, plural, or qualified by a genus-level modifier, if it is anchored to one concrete clinical entity:

- **(a) Clinical-state anchor.** The mention names the diagnosis, presenting condition, or other clinical state that a case-specific predicate attributes to an identified individual, or to an identified family, kindred, or proband group. Typical cues are finding or presentation verbs (“affected by,” “diagnosed with,” “presenting with/as”) and “patient(s) / proband(s) / kindred(s) / male(s) / female(s) with” phrases.  
  - Example: “a son affected by Norrie disease” → “Norrie disease” as **Specific Disease**.
- **(b) Gene or locus anchor.** The mention is anchored by a gene symbol or locus name that designates one disorder, and its head names the disorder itself. Strings whose head denotes a genotype or molecular entity (e.g., *mutation*, *full mutation*, *allele*, *variant*, *expansion*, *gene*, *protein*) are not disease mentions and are not labeled under this clause (e.g., “FMR1 full mutations,” “NDP gene”).

The label remains **Disease Class** for:
- unanchored umbrella or taxonomic terms (e.g., “autosomal recessive disease”);
- generic phenotypic features listed in a general description of a disorder and not attributed to any particular subject (e.g., “mental retardation” in “…is characterized by ichthyosis, mental retardation, spasticity…”; see Example 11).

Relative-directed mentions (e.g., “family history of ovarian cancer”) attribute the disease to unidentified relatives. They are not anchors under clause (a) and are annotated as **Modifiers** (Section 3).

These clauses decide only the Specific Disease vs. Disease Class label. They do not change span boundaries. Modifier status is decided first (Section 3): a disease span that is a prenominal modifier (e.g., “ovarian cancer cases”) remains **Modifier** and is not reclassified under clause (a).

#### Deficiency-type nominals with a molecular complement
A *deficiency-type nominal* is a head noun denoting a loss-of-function state (*deficiency*, *absence*, or *deficient activity*, with any adjectival premodifier) whose complement names a gene, enzyme, protein, or complement component. Rule A (the narrower rule) is applied first. Rule B (the broader rule) explicitly excludes every construction that Rule A governs.

**Rule A: Compact-unit boundary (span rule).** When a construction contains a compact molecular label (a gene symbol or abbreviation, as a premodifier or in parentheses) together with a spelled-out name, a parenthetical gloss, or an *of*-phrase that elaborates the same molecule, the **Specific Disease** span is the compact label plus the head noun. Exclude the spelled-out name, the parenthetical gloss, and the elaborating *of*-phrase.
- “…is not sufficient to cause G6PD deficiency” → “G6PD deficiency.”
- If a parenthetical separates the spelled-out name from the head (“glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency”), no contiguous compact unit exists. The interrupted string is not annotated as one mention (see What NOT 6).

**Rule B: Loss-of-state label (label rule).** Applies when the molecular complement is expressed as an *of*-phrase, or as a premodifier not covered by Rule A. Label the whole nominal, from the head through the end of the prepositional complement, as **Specific Disease** when all of the following hold:
1. The head is a loss-of-state nominal.
2. The nominal denotes an inherited or clinical condition of a **subject**, meaning a case-specific individual, family, kindred, or proband group. Characterizing or etiological predicates (“caused by,” “characterized by”) signal such a condition only when the subject is case-specific.
3. The complement is a gene, enzyme, protein, or complement component.

The preposition does not block or shorten the span.

Rule B does **not** apply when:
- (a) the head is a measured quantity (activity, level, concentration) and no deficiency-type term expresses a loss-of-function state (e.g., “reduction of FALDH activity”);
- (b) the complement is a non-protein entity such as a nutrient, metabolite, toxin, or environmental agent;
- (c) the nominal describes an assay, experimental manipulation, or in-vitro condition (e.g., an enzyme variant characterized in an expression system) rather than a subject’s state;
- (d) the head is a bare symptom or sign noun (see What NOT 7);
- (e) the complement is only a protein or gene name with no deficiency state expressed; the complement alone is not labeled;
- (f) the nominal is a feature in a general description of a disorder (e.g., “X is characterized by … deficient activity of Y”) rather than a case-specific subject;
- (g) the nominal is a molecular or tissue-level consequence proposed as a mechanism (e.g., “resulting in absence of the FMR1 protein,” “results in a deficiency of norrin within the developing retina”);
- (h) the nominal is a population-level statement (e.g., “the high prevalence of … deficiency in African populations”);
- (i) the form is adjectival or participial (e.g., “G6PD deficient”), which is a **Modifier** (Section 3).

**Shared rules for deficiency-type nominals:**
- The label is the same whether the molecular name is a premodifier or sits inside a prepositional complement. A named disorder in premodifier form (e.g., “C7 deficiency,” “G6PD deficiency”) is a **Specific Disease** under Section 1 without further analysis.
- When the molecular name is spelled out without any abbreviation, the head noun alone is never labeled (a bare *deficiency* is a general term; see What NOT 4). The whole nominal is labeled under Rule B if its conditions hold.
- Nosological heads end the span at the head. When a name’s head is nosological (an eponym, *disease*, *syndrome*, or an *-osis* term) and the gene or protein appears as a separate phrase outside the name, do not extend the span. For example, “Coats’ disease of the retina … caused by somatic mutation in the NDP gene” is annotated as “Coats’ disease” only.
- If the gold annotations for a construction already include the expansion or *of*-phrase, the gold boundaries govern.

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

**Prenominal disease names.** A multiword disease name, or an abbreviation whose expansion names a disease defined earlier in the text, that directly precedes a head noun is annotated as **Modifier**. The head noun and any following nomenclature tokens are left unannotated. This applies whether the head denotes a clinical or population entity (e.g., “ovarian cancer cases,” “fragile X syndrome patients”) or a gene, protein, or gene symbol (e.g., “ancient SLS genes,” where “SLS” is defined earlier in the abstract; “breast cancer susceptibility gene 1,” where “breast cancer” is the Modifier and “susceptibility gene 1” is not annotated). Do not infer that the disease span is O merely because the whole noun phrase names a gene or protein. Adjectival forms such as “G6PD deficient phenotype” are also **Modifiers** (see Section 1, rule B(i)).

**Exclusions.** The prenominal rule does not apply when:
- (a) the disease span is itself the syntactic head of the phrase (e.g., “Coats disease”);
- (b) the span is separated from its head by a preposition, relative clause, or intervening parenthetical;
- (c) the span is a non-disease attributive term, such as an anatomical, chemical, or organism name;
- (d) a gene symbol merely resembles a disease abbreviation and no disease expansion is established in the surrounding context.

**Relative-directed phrases.** Disease mentions in “family history of X” or “relatives with X” describe unidentified relatives rather than an identified subject, and are annotated as **Modifier**. A disease attributed to the identified subject (e.g., “patients with X,” “affected by X”) is a **Specific Disease** under Section 1.

**Precedence.** Modifier status is decided before the Specific Disease vs. Disease Class label. A span annotated as **Modifier** is not relabeled under Section 1.

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease. For deficiency-type nominals with a molecular complement, the compact-unit rule (Rule A) and the loss-of-state rule (Rule B) in Section 1 define the span.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

### Precedence when rules overlap
1. **Modifier status first (Section 3).** Prenominal disease names, including those in gene nomenclature, and relative-directed phrases are Modifiers before any other label is considered.
2. **Specific Disease vs. Disease Class for remaining spans (Section 1).** Anchor clauses decide the label only. They never change span boundaries.
3. **Deficiency-type nominals.** Rule A (compact unit) is applied before Rule B (loss-of-state label). Rule B does not apply to any construction that Rule A governs. A molecular-complement span is never shortened to the bare head noun.
4. **Negative rules last.** The exclusions in “What NOT to Annotate” apply after the positive labels above. The only exception is the grey-area Disease Class terms described in Example 11.

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**  
   - Very general terms such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” etc. were excluded when used alone.  
   - A bare head such as “deficiency” or “absence” is never annotated on its own. Deficiency-type nominals that name a molecule or disease are governed by the rules under *Deficiency-type nominals with a molecular complement* (Section 1).  
   - However, the terms “cancer” and “tumor” were retained.

5. **Do not annotate references to **biological processes**  
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis.”

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule. This includes a parenthetical gloss or expansion placed between a molecular name and its head (see Section 1, Rule A).  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

7. **Do not annotate symptoms or signs (symptom convention)**  
   - Bare symptom or sign nouns and phenotypic findings (e.g., “ichthyosis,” “spasticity,” “exudative retinal detachment”) are not annotated as **Specific Disease**.  
   - Phenotypic terms are annotated only as **Disease Class**, and only when they are grey-area terms under Example 11 (e.g., “mental retardation” in a general disorder description).

**NEW MODERATION PRINCIPLES:**
### 1. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: O (Missed) (8 cases)
- **Principle (Anchor-Free Compound Disease Names):** IF a noun phrase uses a generic pathology, defect, or functional-deficit head (a non-anchor noun that denotes a condition or deficit state rather than a named disorder) together with a premodifier or postmodifier naming an anatomical site or a gene or enzyme, AND the phrase is either (a) introduced or restated as a term through a metalinguistic marker (a "so-called" gloss, a parenthetical, or an appositive to an already-named disorder), or (b) structured as a defect head followed by a prepositional complement naming a gene or enzyme, where the phrase denotes a heritable condition, THEN label the entire contiguous span as SpecificDisease, including any internal parenthetical acronym. Do not split the anatomical or gene/enzyme token into a separate entity, and do not default the span to O.
  - **Negative constraint:** Do NOT apply this rule when (1) the span already contains a nomenclature anchor (an eponym, a disease-class head such as "disease" or "syndrome," or an established acronym), in which case standard labeling governs; (2) the head noun denotes a measured value, lab result, or transient finding in a results or methods statement and does not name a condition; (3) the phrase refers to a category of conditions rather than one named condition, which remains DiseaseClass; (4) the gene or enzyme token is an assay, reagent, or method rather than the complement specifying the defect; or (5) the phrase is a plain anatomical description with no metalinguistic gloss and no defect-to-condition naming relationship.

### 2. Gold: DiseaseClass vs LLM: O (Missed) (5 cases)
- **Principle: Pathological-State Spans in Disease-Characterization Frames**
  - **IF** a span, taken as a whole, denotes a pathological condition, clinical sign, symptom, lesion, or deficit state (its head is a state noun, or a non-state noun qualified by an abnormality-denoting modifier), **AND** the span is an argument of a predicate that characterizes, causes, or results from a SpecificDisease mention (or is a conjunct of such an argument in a coordinated list),
  - **THEN** label the span `DiseaseClass`, and label each parallel conjunct in the same coordinated list that also meets the head-noun condition.
  - **Negative constraint (do NOT apply when):**
    - the head denotes an anatomical structure, a gene, a gene product or molecule, a substance or fluid, or a procedure, even inside a characterization frame;
    - the span is itself disease nomenclature (an eponym or an "X disease/syndrome/disorder" name), which is `SpecificDisease`;
    - the span is a bare modifier or a composite name that does not itself name a pathological state (`Modifier` or `CompositeMention`), so coordination must not merge conjuncts into a composite span or relabel a `SpecificDisease`;
    - the predicate is only a locative or temporal adjunct rather than a characterizing or causal argument.
    - Surface frame, list position, or familiarity with the term does not substitute for the head-noun test. The head-noun test is required in every case.

### 3. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: DiseaseClass (Label Mismatch) (2 cases)
- **Individuating Anchor Rule (Generic-Head Disease Phrases):**
  - **IF** a disease noun phrase has a generic category head (e.g., a noun for a broad disease type such as *infection*, *inflammation*, *syndrome*, or *disorder*) **and** that head is preceded by a premodifier naming a causal agent (organism, gene, toxin) or an anatomic locus, **THEN** label the entire phrase **SpecificDisease**, because the anchor fixes one nosological entity. The same applies when the phrase contains a proper name, eponym, or acronym. This rule governs only the label of the disease span.
  - **Does NOT apply** when: (a) the only premodifiers are course, severity, or timing descriptors; (b) the head is unmodified or functions as an umbrella or phenotype term, especially in a symptom or feature list; or (c) the causal or anatomic element is not a premodifier within the same noun phrase. In these cases, label **DiseaseClass**.

### 4. Gold: CompositeMention vs LLM: Modifier (Label Mismatch) (1 cases)
- **Coordinated Disease-Span Rule:** IF a disease-name span contains a coordinator (conjunction, disjunction, or slash-type delimiter) that links two or more distinct disease names or disease-site specifiers under one head noun, whether explicit or elided, THEN label the entire span **CompositeMention**, regardless of its syntactic slot (prepositional complement of a frame phrase, pre-nominal attributive position, or argument position). Reserve **Modifier** for spans where exactly one disease name functions pre-nominally before a non-disease head noun, with no coordinated disease referent in the span.
  - **Negative constraint:** Do NOT apply this rule when the coordinated elements are not disease names or disease-site specifiers (e.g., coordinated drugs, anatomical structures, symptoms, or procedures), when the coordinator belongs to a single fixed disease-name unit that denotes one disease entity, or when the coordinated items are aliases for the same disease. In those cases, the single-disease classification applies. A surrounding frame such as "family history of" or "risk of" must not determine the label on its own.
  - **Generalization:** The trigger is the structural presence of two distinct disease referents bound by a coordinator under a shared head, not any specific lexical item (e.g., "breast," "ovarian," "cancer") or any particular frame. The rule applies to any coordinator and any disease-type or disease-site pairing.
  - **Consistency:** This keeps CompositeMention for multi-disease spans, Modifier for single-disease attributive uses, and SpecificDisease for standalone disease names, matching the entity definitions.
