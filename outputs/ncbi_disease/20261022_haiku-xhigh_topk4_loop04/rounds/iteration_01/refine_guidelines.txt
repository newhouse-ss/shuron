# Biomedical Annotation Guidelines

## What to Annotate

### 1. Annotate all **Specific Disease** mentions
A textual string referring to a disease name may refer to a **Specific Disease** or a **Disease Class**.  
- **Disease Class**: Mentions that could be described as a family of multiple specific diseases.  
- **Specific Disease**: Mentions that can be linked to one specific definition that does not include further categorization.

**Example**  
> Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disease characterized by short stature, very short limbs and joint problems that restrict mobility.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “Diastrophic dysplasia” as **Specific Disease**  
  - “autosomal recessive disease” as **Disease Class**

---

### 2. Annotate **contiguous text strings**
A textual string may refer to two or more separate disease mentions. Such mentions are annotated with the **Composite Mention** category.

**Example**  
> The text phrase “Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy” refers to two separate diseases. If this phrase is separated into two strings: “Duchenne” and “Becker muscular dystrophy,” it results in information loss, because the word “Duchenne” on its own is not a disease mention.

---

### 3. Annotate disease mentions that are used as **Modifiers** for other concepts
A textual string may refer to a disease name, but it may modify a noun phrase (or may not be a noun phrase itself). In such cases, annotate using the **Modifier** category.

**Example**  
> Although this mutation was initially detected in four of 33 colorectal cancer families analysed from eastern England, more extensive analysis has reduced the frequency to four of 52 English HNPCC kindreds analysed.

- **Annotate**:  
  - “colorectal cancer” as **Modifier**  
  - “HNPCC” as **Modifier**

---

### 4. Annotate **duplicate mentions**
For each sentence in the PubMed abstract and title, the locations of all disease mentions are marked, including duplicates within the same sentence.

---

### 5. Annotate **minimum necessary span of text**
Use the smallest span necessary to include all tokens expressing the most specific form of the disease.

**Example**  
> In the case of “insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus,” the disease mention including the whole phrase was preferred over its substrings such as “diabetes mellitus” or “diabetes.”

---

### 6. Annotate all **synonymous mentions**
Abbreviation definitions such as “Huntington disease” (“HD”) are separated into two annotated mentions (one for the long form, one for the abbreviation).

---

## What NOT to Annotate

1. **Do not annotate organism names**  
   - Organism names such as “human” were excluded from the preferred mention.  
   - Viruses, bacteria, and other organism names were not annotated **unless** it was clear that the disease caused by these organisms is being discussed.  
   - **Example**  
     > Studies of biopsied tissue for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus were negative.  
     
     In this case: “Epstein-Barr virus” and “cytomegalovirus” are annotated as **Specific Disease** category **only** if the context clearly indicates the diseases they cause, otherwise not.

2. **Do not annotate gender**  
   - Tokens such as “male” and “female” were only included if they specifically identified a new form of the disease.  
   - **Example**  
     > “male breast cancer”

3. **Do not annotate overlapping mentions**  
   - **Example**: The phrase “von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease” is annotated as **one single disease mention** (Specific Disease category).

4. **Do not annotate general terms**  
   - Very general terms such as “disease,” “syndrome,” “deficiency,” “complications,” “abnormalities,” etc. were excluded.  
   - However, the terms “cancer” and “tumor” were retained.

5. **Do not annotate references to **biological processes**  
   - e.g. Terms corresponding to biological processes such as “tumorigenesis” or “cancerogenesis.”

6. **Do not annotate disease mentions interrupted by nested mentions**  
   - Essentially, do not break the contiguous text rule.  
   - **Example**  
     > WT1 dysfunction is implicated in both neoplastic (Wilms tumor, mesothelioma, leukemia, and breast cancer) and nonneoplastic (glomerulosclerosis) disease.  
     
     Here, the mentions “neoplastic disease” and “nonneoplastic disease” are not annotated because other tokens break up these phrases.

**NEW MODERATION PRINCIPLES:**
### 1. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: O (Missed) (5 cases)
- **Deficiency-Nominal Disease Naming:**
  - **IF** a noun phrase is headed by a deficiency-type noun (e.g., "deficiency," "deficient activity") that takes a postnominal prepositional complement (of/in) naming an enzyme, protein, or gene, **or** is a short-form re-mention of such a condition (a gene or enzyme abbreviation plus the deficiency head), **and** the phrase names a pathological condition that defines or causes a disease,
  - **THEN** tag the full noun phrase, including the enzyme or gene complement, as **SpecificDisease**. Tag any later coreferent short-form mention of the same condition as **SpecificDisease**, not O. The complement is part of the disease name, not a separate biochemical description.
  - **Negative Constraint:** Do NOT apply this rule when the deficiency-type word is attributive (an adjective or premodifier describing a noun, such as "deficient patients" or "G6PD-deficient phenotype"). Tag those as **Modifier**. Also do NOT apply it to single symptom words (e.g., "fever"), which follow the separate DiseaseClass convention, or to phrases where the deficiency describes a measured quantity in a sample rather than a named condition.

### 2. Gold: SpecificDisease vs LLM: DiseaseClass (Label Mismatch) (5 cases)
- **Individuated-Referent Test for Disease Mentions:** IF a disease mention (a) contains a proper-name, eponym, acronym, gene-symbol, or enzyme-defined anchor in its head or immediate modifier, regardless of grammatical number, OR (b) is an unanchored head noun, with or without adjectival modifiers, that names the diagnosed or presenting condition of a specific individual in the text (e.g., a participial "presenting with…" phrase, a "found to have…" construction, or a clausal subject/predicate describing one patient), THEN label the head disease mention SpecificDisease. IF an unanchored head noun instead occurs in a generic frame, such as a syndrome-level list of characteristic features ("characterized by…"), or an enumerated differential introduced by a comparative construction ("other causes of…"), THEN label it DiseaseClass.
  - **Negative constraint:** Do not promote an unanchored term to SpecificDisease solely because a patient appears elsewhere in the document; the clause frame containing the term governs. Do not treat plural number or added modifiers as evidence of a class label when an anchor is present. Modifiers alone never change the label, which is determined by the disease head noun. Anchored terms that name a broad grouping of distinct disorders rather than one disease entity remain DiseaseClass.

### 3. Gold: DiseaseClass vs LLM: O (Missed) (3 cases)
- **Disease-Manifestation Nominal Rule:** IF a noun phrase denotes an abnormal pathological state, developmental abnormality, clinical sign, or deficiency of a disease-relevant molecule, AND it is the argument of a characterization, causation, or etiology predicate within a clause that describes a SpecificDisease (directly, or through a chain of causal predicates), OR it is a coordinated member of such an argument list, THEN label the entire nominal, including its head, internal modifiers, and any prepositional complement, as one DiseaseClass span, even when the phrase has no explicit disease head word. Label each coordinated member as its own span.
  - **Does NOT apply when:** (a) the phrase is itself a named disease, syndrome, or nosological label; (b) the clause's causal chain concerns a gene, drug, exposure, or population without characterizing a disease; (c) the phrase is a study variable, outcome measure, assay, method, or procedure; (d) it describes neutral physiological or molecular background outside any disease-characterizing clause; or (e) it is a standalone severity, onset, or temporal qualifier, which remains a Modifier rather than part of a DiseaseClass span.

### 4. Gold: Modifier vs LLM: O (Missed) (2 cases)
- **Core Principle (Disease Premodifier of a Locus-Designator Head):** IF a disease-denoting premodifier (a spelled-out disease name, or an acronym the document has already defined as a disease) appears in attributive position directly before a nominal head that denotes a genetic locus or gene-level entity (e.g., "gene," "genes," or "gene" followed by a numeral or symbol), THEN tag the disease premodifier as **Modifier**. The locus-designator head does not convert the disease span to O. The locus designator and any bare gene symbol remain O.
  - **Negative Constraint:** Do NOT apply this rule when (a) the disease term is the head of the phrase or sits in a postmodifying prepositional phrase (e.g., "gene for ___"), since the disease is then not attributive; (b) the acronym has no disease definition or established disease referent in the document, so it remains O; (c) the span is a gene symbol or locus name containing no disease term; or (d) the premodifier is a non-disease attributive noun (e.g., anatomical, drug, or procedure terms).
  - **Validity Check:** Consistent with the entity definitions. Modifier is applied to a disease-denoting span that attributively qualifies another entity. Gene symbols stay outside the schema, and SpecificDisease and DiseaseClass boundaries are unchanged for non-attributive uses.
